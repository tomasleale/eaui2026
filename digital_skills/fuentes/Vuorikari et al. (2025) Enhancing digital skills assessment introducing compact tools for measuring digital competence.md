Enhancing digital skills assessment: introducing compact
tools for measuring digital competence

Riina Vuorikari1,2 · Artur Pokropek3 · Jonatan Castaño Muñoz4

Accepted: 30 January 2025 / Published online: 19 February 2025
© The Author(s) 2025

Abstract
Today, individuals interact with digital technologies in a plethora of ways requiring a range
of  digital  skills,  knowledge  and  attitudes—a  combination  that  forms  digital  competence.
Traditional  digital  skills  measurements  are  often  lengthy  and  complex,  hindering  real-
world applications. Therefore, this study addresses the need for efficient digital competence
measurement tools by developing and validating two concise instruments aligned with the
Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp). Mini-21 offers an instrument that
covers all 21 competences and a nano-6 version captures the five core areas of the frame-
work. These instruments enable quick and concise digital competence assessments in vari-
ous settings; for initial screening, diagnostic of low-skilled individuals for targeted educa-
tional interventions, and integration into broader surveys.
Psychometric analyses (confirmatory factor analysis, item response theory methods, meas-
urement invariance, internal consistency and concurrent validity) of 460 respondents from
Latvia  and  Spain  confirm  their  high  validity  and  reliability.  Furthermore,  by  comparing
psychometric models, the study demonstrates the superiority of tools integrating the three
attributes of competence: knowledge, skills, and attitudes. The alignment of our findings
with the European Union’s DigComp framework guarantees their grounding in an estab-
lished theory. It offers practical benefits promoting their immediate application in educa-
tion and training settings, especially where DigComp has been adopted.

1  Introduction

R. Vuorikari et al.

Measuring  digital  skills  is  a  much-discussed  topic  not  least  because  of  its  importance
for citizens to carry out digital activities in today’s world. Various digital technologies
and ICT tools are used for work, for participation in society, for social interactions and
leisure.  Digital  technologies  refer,  among  other  things,  to  Information  and  Communi-
cation  Technologies  (ICT)  and  devices  such  as  personal  computers  and  smartphones
that can be used for communication, for example, via the internet, wireless networks or
mobile networks (henceforth digital tools). In the last 20 years, assessing and measur-
ing  digital  skills  have  become  the  focus  of  a  considerable  amount  of  academic  litera-
ture (which will be reviewed in Sect. 1.2), but also books and institutional reports (e.g.
OECD, 2001; OECD, 2005; UN, 2005; EU, 2006; Ala-Mutka, 2011). In this study, the
focus is on citizens’ digital skills as opposed to the digital skills of a specific segment of
the population such as youth.

Two main characteristics of the digital skills assessment instruments arise from the
reviews published since 2000; the focus on measuring procedural knowledge (e.g. oper-
ational  digital  skills)  and  measuring  knowledge  about  digital  tools  and  their  use  (e.g.
Litt,  2013;  Siddiq  et  al.,  2016).  Today’s  digital  environments,  however,  increasingly
require not only operational skills and knowledge, but also a critical, yet open, attitude.
It  can  lead  to  reflecting  on  existing  technologies  and  consequently,  lead  to  more  ethi-
cal  and  responsible  online  behavior.  Also,  a  more  advanced,  critical  understanding  of
digital environments is needed to help understand how digital technologies function in
socio-economic  terms,  for  example,  why  algorithms  on  digital  platforms  promote  cer-
tain content or why certain advertisements are seen.

Considering these new requirements, the latest generation of self-reporting instruments
for  digital  skills  has  added  new  attributes  to  measurement  tools.  In  this  paper,  the  Dig-
CompSat  tool  is  taken  as  a  case  study  for  an  assessment  instrument  of  citizens’  digital
capacities (Clifford et al., 2020). DigCompSat is an instrument derived from the conceptual
reference model called the Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (hereinafter: Dig-
Comp). It offers agreed definitions of  attributes  of  digital competence (knowledge,  skills
and  attitudes)  as  well  as  a  set  of  vocabulary  to  create  a  common  understanding  of  what
digital competence entails based on Key Competences for Lifelong Learning (EC, 2019).

The  DigCompSat  tool  is  targeted  to  the  general  population,  “everyday  users”;  with
its  82  items,  any  citizen  can  use  it  to  assess  and  better  understand  the  extent  of  their
digital  capacities.  In  addition  to  covering  individuals’  skills  and  knowledge,  it  also
includes attitudes regarding the use of digital technologies. The psychometric properties
for internal consistency are high; Cronbach’s alpha is at 0.987 for the whole question-
naire while the Cronbach alpha values in each DigComp area range from 0.907 to 0.944
(Clifford  et  al.,  2020).  Moreover,  the  validity  of  the  items  was  guaranteed  via  experts
and user consultations.

Similar new attributes can be found in another instrument, however, with a different
target audience (youth); Helsper et al. (2020) make a distinction between users (1) being
able to use the functionalities of digital technologies (i.e. skills), (2) understanding why
they are designed in certain ways (i.e. knowledge), and (3) being able to use that knowl-
edge in managing interactions in digital spaces (critical aspects). It can be argued that
integrating  new  attributes  like  these  into  measurement  tools  better  takes  into  account
the multi-layered nature of digital competence and its construct, thus adding richness to
self-reporting instruments.

521

Such  new  attributes,  however,  also  bring  along  a  drawback  of  increased  complex-
ity. Valid and reliable measurement tools for digital competence are already usually very
comprehensive and long, and whereas they serve well their primary purpose, they may be
inadequate for other goals due to their complexity. For instance, a long questionnaire can
be a heavy exercise for an initial quick diagnosis of competence gaps and training needs
for populations with low-skills. Also, completing a long list of items can lead to discour-
agement fatigue, and eventually to response bias and poorer quality data. Last, a lengthy
version of the questionnaire can be too long to be integrated with other research question-
naires  where  digital  skills  are  only  a  subpart  of  the  research  goals.  Therefore,  creating  a
short version of the instrument, which maintains most of the information, is often consid-
ered a good practice in the field (e.g. Hargittai & Hsieh, 2012; Helsper et al., 2020).

1.1   Aim of the paper and the rationale to focus on DigCompSat

Taking  the  DigCompSat  tool  as  a  case  study,  the  goal  of  this  paper  is  to  propose  two
shorter versions of self-reporting instruments for digital competence while still keeping a
rich approach to digital competence measurement by focusing on the attributes of knowl-
edge, skills and attitudes. To our knowledge, this is the first study to combine the reduction
of scale with these three attributes. Namely, the goal is to:

a)  Respect and integrate the key elements identified by recent approaches to digital com-

petence (i.e. knowledge, skills and attitudes); and

b)  Keep the wide spectrum of competences within the domain of digital competence (as

is described in the DigComp framework).

In DigCompSat, a total of 82 items were developed to allow individuals regardless of
their  background  to  get  a  detailed  evaluation  of  their  digital  skills,  knowledge  and  atti-
tudes. Through self-reflection, DigCompSat also allows users to become aware of different
areas of digital competence as they are defined in the DigComp framework (Clifford et al.,
2020). Today, the item set is implemented in websites such as MyDigiSkills.eu and Skil-
lify, and it is used in large-scale surveys, e.g. Germany (Stürz et al., 2022) and later in 6
European countries (Stürz et al., 2023).

The fact that DigCompSat, as an assessment tool for an individual’s digital capacities,
is theoretically aligned with the Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp)
offers  an  important  rationale  for  this  research.  The  conceptual  model  of  the  DigComp
framework identifies 21 competences in 5 main areas: information literacy, communication
& collaboration, digital content, safety and problem solving (Fig. 1). It was first published
by  Ferrari  (2013)  with  the  latest  update  being  released  in  March  2022  (Vuorikari  et  al.,
2022b).

During  the  last  decade,  DigComp  has  become  a  key  part  of  education  ecosystems
(Vuorikari et al., 2022a); it is used to support curriculum planning, instruction and assess-
ment both at the EU level and in Member States (EC, 2019). Already five years after the
first publication, 38 case studies were shared to describe how DigComp was being imple-
mented  in  many  parts  of  Europe  (Kluzer  &  Pujol,  2018)  emphasizing  the  importance  of
learning  from  one  another  and  inspiring  a  community  of  practitioners.  DigComp  also
supports  policy  monitoring  on  digital  upskilling,  the  Digital  Skills  Indicator  2.0  tracks
levels  of  digital  skills  in  EU-27  (Vuorikari  et  al.,  2022a).  This  comprehensive  reference

The  second  important  rationale  for  focusing  on  DigCompSat  in  this  research  is  that
including attitudes in the assessment process is driven by the interest in improving users’
online  behavior.  The  theory  of  planned  behavior,  which  links  beliefs  to  behavior,  can  be
used to explain this space: it signals attitudes as a key attribute for explaining why indi-
viduals  do,  or  do  not,  take  action  and  behave  in  the  way  they  have  rationally  planned  or
know is important (Ajzen, 1991). An example of this can be privacy-compromising online
behavior related to the use of cookies. In 2021, 80% of adult internet users in EU-27 said
they know that cookies can be used to track the movements of people on the internet. How-
ever, only 36% had changed the settings in their internet browser to prevent or limit cookie
use.1 The low number of those taking action might be attributed to the lack of skills, but
also to the lack of motivation to actually act upon one’s knowledge in this area (i.e. atti-
tude). Having tools to better understand this space is becoming important.

Another argument for adding more aspects of users’ attitudes in a self-assessment tool
is to improve the pedagogical potential of the self-assessment exercises. We identify three
issues.  Firstly,  the  process  of  self-assessment  can  lead  to  thinking  about  one’s  learning

and therefore promote active learning and formative assessment (Bonwell & Eison, 1991;
Dochy  et  al.,  1999;  Prince,  2004).  To  increase  the  value  and  exhaustivity  of  self-assess-
ment instruments, it is important that they  cover all three domains  of educational activi-
ties  outlined  in  Bloom’s  taxonomy:  cognitive,  affective,  and  psychomotor  (Anderson  &
Krathwohl, 2001). Whereas the latter is concerned with skills, the cognitive domain relates
to knowledge, and the affective domain to growth in feelings or emotional areas, in other
words, attitudes. Secondly, there is a pedagogical value of self-assessment as a reflective
tool; it can help users understand their strengths and weaknesses, but it can also play a pos-
itive role in mitigating biases in self-reported data, for example, by reducing social desir-
ability bias. Last, compared to traditional testing, Allen and van der Velden (2005) signal
that self-assessments can allow individuals to take into consideration information about the
subjects that are difficult to measure in tests in an objective way – and a clear example of
this could be attitudes towards the digital world.

With all its strengths outlined above, the main drawback of the DigCompSat tool is that
by covering extensively the DigComp model (21 competences), it also makes it long and
time-consuming (e.g. median response time for DigCompSat pilot was 23 min). These are
factors  that  reduce  its  value  for  some  other  tasks  than  a  comprehensive  self-assessment.
In  this  paper,  we  work  based  on  two  real-world  scenarios:  a  need  for  a  short,  diagnostic
version  of  the  scale  when  the  goal  is  to  quickly  assess  the  overall  level  focusing  on  all
21 DigComp competences. This could be, for example, for screening applicants for train-
ing or career guidance, or for identification of digital competence development needs for a
more comprehensive training intervention. The second scenario outlines a need for an even
shorter version focusing only on 5 main areas, it could be suitable in scenarios when the
scale is to be included in other surveys where digital skills are not the main focus and thus
have only a limited possibility to include numerous items.

Our study is methodologically akin to research by Cachia et al. (2024) which involved
similar  processing  of  items.  However,  our  focus  is  on  different  context,  dataset  and  tool.
While Cachia et al. (2024) aim to reduce the complexity of a tool for educational institu-
tions to collectively assess where they stand with learning in the digital age, our focus is
on a tool for the digital competence of individuals such as citizens. Moreover, as the focus
of DigCompSat is on individuals (and not organizations), in our study an additional level
of analysis comes from adding the attributes of knowledge, skills and attitudes which are
essential components in competence definition.

1.2   Review of previous studies measuring digital skills in general population

Much research in the past 20 years has focused on constructing instruments to assess and
monitor  individuals’  digital  capacities.  In  general,  instruments  that  measure  digital  skills
and competence use either direct or indirect measurement or a combination of the two. In
the  following,  we  first  review  literature  that  focuses  on  indirect  measurement  tools,  e.g.
individuals  self-reporting  on  their  digital  activities,  estimating  their  mastery  of  certain
tasks, and then the focus moves on tools using more direct methods. We also cover issues
of dimensionality of the construct, and the reliability and validity of instruments in a wider
context. Importantly, in this review, the focus is on tools targeted to the digital skills of the
general population, e.g. “everyday users”. Therefore, it excludes digital skills tools in edu-
cational settings, e.g. the International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS)
by Fraillon (2020); Siddiq et al. (2016) and for higher education (Lucas et al., 2022; Sillat

1.2.1   Three generations of indirect methods of measurement

Generation 1 One of the earlier reviews on the measurement of everyday users’ basic inter-
net skills is by Litt (2013) who studied 16 instruments, some of which date back as far as
2002 (Hargittai, 2002). Half of the instruments used indirect methods such as self-report-
ing  methods  focusing  on  operational  skills  and  information  seeking,  in  addition  to  using
communication tools such as email. The questions included both skills (e.g. “I can use the
reply  features  for  email”)  and  knowledge  type  (“I  know  what  a  browser  is”).  Such  ques-
tions aim to measure outcomes indirectly and consider them proxies for one’s capacity.

In the following years, many indirect digital skills instruments asked individuals to self-
report their digital activities. In this area, one of the widely used tools is called the Digital
Skills Indicator (DSI). It targets individuals aged 16–74 in all EU countries, using repre-
sentative samples at the national level through the ICT Usage in Households and by Indi-
viduals, and it is computed by Eurostat. The DSI was first piloted in 2014 and currently,
DSI 2.0 focuses on 30 activities carried out using digital technologies. The DSI 2.0 item
set is of high psychometric quality; both at the individual item level and at the scale level
(Cronbach’s alpha 0.902) (Vuorikari et al., 2022a). The DSI is a criteria-based assessment
tool, the basic level of digital skills is defined as having at least one activity in each of its
5  areas.  The  Sustainable  Development  Goal  4.4.1,  “Proportion  of  youth  and  adults  with
information and communications technology (ICT) skills, by type of skill”2 is derived from
this method.

Already  early  on,  researchers  started  noticing  caveats  in  their  tool  design  based  on
self-reporting; van Deursen and van Dijk (2010) noted that people often overestimated or
underestimated  their  expected  performance.  Often  statements  were  also  misdirected,  e.g.
focusing on the frequency of carrying out activities rather than the capability itself. Also,
self-reporting based on Likert-scale answers was found to be imprecise “because there is
no assumption that the different positions on the scale are equally spaced” (Aesaert et al.,
2014).

Helsper et al. (2020), through a rigorous review referring to previous literature in Hel-
sper et al. (2020) and van Deursen et al. (2016), gathered “seven common sins in survey
items  designed  to  measure  skills”.  They  include  issues  such  that  items  should  ask  about
possessing a certain digital skill (e.g. Can do; I know how to do) rather than about usage
(e.g. Have you used, Do you use) or how expert users consider themselves in relation to a
certain  skill  (e.g.  How  good  are  you  at/  rate  yourself  on  the  following?).  Importantly,  to
avoid  “confidence”  bias  or  social  desirability  bias,  Haddon  et  al.  (2020,  p.  111)  suggest
that, when it comes to the decision about how to operationalize skills, more factual ques-
tions (“I know how to…”) are preferable to self-evaluative questions (“I am good at…”)
because they introduce less measurement bias and help distinguish digital skills from dig-
ital  self-efficacy.  (Note:  stemming  from  earlier  research  that  measures  self-efficacy,  it  is
important to acknowledge that tools to measure digital self-efficacy also exist, e.g. Ulfert-
Blank & Schmidt, 2022a, 2022b).

Generation 2 To create a measurement instrument that avoids the known caveats of self-
reporting tools mentioned above, van Deursen et al. (2016) created a valid and reliable self-
reporting  survey  instrument  called  the  Internet  Skills  Scale  (ISS)  focusing  on  the  general
population. The novel contribution of the ISS scale was that the response items were made
to focus on truth claims (‘Not at all true of me’, ‘Mostly true of me’) which, authors argue,
may invite a more neutral and objective response from participants. In other words, the focus
on new types of response items was created to avoid socially desirable responding (SDR), the
tendency for people to present a favorable image of themselves on questionnaires.

The ISS instrument consists of five areas of internet skills: operational, navigation informa-
tion, social, creative, and mobile. In addition to being validated by using a representative sam-
ple survey of Dutch Internet users, the instrument was validated in a cross-national context,
e.g. by UK internet users (Helsper et al., 2015), in Italy (Surian & Sciandra, 2019) and in Slo-
venia (Grošelj et al., 2021), a country that has lower levels of internet penetration. The meas-
urement tool has been extensively used to measure and model digital engagement (DiSTO,
2023). We call this the second generation of indirect instruments to measure the digital skills
of the general population as they were built on experiences gained from previous work and
therefore significantly enhanced (see Table 1).

Generation  3  More  recently,  a  third  generation  of  instruments  has  emerged  which  takes
a broader view of the construct of digital competence and the variety of its attributes. Dig-
CompSat is a good example where the assessment items reflect the attributes of knowledge,
skills  and  attitudes,  thus  adding  more  to  the  construct  of  digital  competence.  For  example,
the DigComp competence of “evaluating content on the internet” focuses on misinformation:
its knowledge item is about awareness of misinformation “I know that some information on
the Internet is false”, a skill’s item is about knowing how to address the issue “I know how to
identify the purpose of an online information source (e.g. to inform, influence, entertain, or
sell)” and finally, an attitude item focuses on motivation to act upon one’s knowledge and use
the skills purposefully “I critically check if the information I find online is reliable”. Also, the
response items are crafted to fit the attribute in question; the skill items’ progression follows
the level of autonomy; at a lower level indicating “I can do it with help” and at the highest
level “I can do it with confidence and, if needed, I can support/ guide others”. Table 2 presents
all the response options that reflect the attributes of knowledge, skills and attitudes. The design
follows good practices to avoid risks related to social desirability (Muszyński et al., 2023).

Another example is a further development of the above-mentioned ISS instrument, Helsper
et al. (2020) created an indicator on youth digital skills (ySkills). Even if the target audience
differs from this research, its design is novel. The 31-item instrument integrates new attributes
that focus separately on skills and aspects of critical knowledge in managing interactions in
digital spaces. The instrument covers four areas: technical and operational skills; information
navigation  and  processing  skills;  communication  and  interaction  skill;  and  content  creation
and production skills. The areas have high internal reliability with Cronbach’s alphas between
0.79 and 0.81. The instrument also follows good practices from the field to avoid the known
caveats  of  self-assessment  tools,  such  as  risks  related  to  social  desirability  (Haddon  et  al.,
2020, p. 28; Helsper et al., 2020, p.19).

Even  if  task-based  tests  are  considered  more  reliable,  they  are  not  free  of  drawbacks.
The items are context specific and they rarely focus on users’ attitudes. Performance tests
are also not always as novel as they may sound. A large-scale review by Siddiq et al. (2016)
looked at 33 performance-based assessment tools for school students’ ICT literacy. It found
that the majority of the tests where static, for example, with standardized multiple-choice
tests that included given response options. In more dynamic ones the test-takers were inter-
acting with the test environments to find the right answer which was given as a selected
response. Hence, even if the tasks might have been performed with the idea that students
are  given  the  opportunity  to  demonstrate  their  knowledge  and  skills,  students  were  still
expected to interact in a more traditional format.

Task-based tests are also costly to develop and deploy. For example, the price of PIAAC
in 25 countries was reported at EUR 23 295 442 (OECD, 2019). For International Com-
puter and Information Literacy Study, the U.S. Department of Education estimated the cost
for conducting the ICILS 2023 round to be $2,894,802 over a 3-year period which includes
both direct and indirect costs (NCES, 2022).

2.1   Sample and participants

The  final  validation  of  the  DigCompSat  tool  took  place  during  four  weeks  in  July  2020.
Two  pilots  took  place  in  Latvia  and  Spain.  A  total  of  460  completed  surveys  were  veri-
fied for which demographic data was correctly gathered and who completed the survey in
a time deemed feasible (Table 4). Data was collecting using a Moodle platform. With its
multilingual user interface, it also allowed monitoring survey progress and time spent as
well as collecting and exporting the data for further analyses. Clifford et al. (2020) detail
the pilots (see especially Chapter 5 and Annex 7). However, due to restrictions imposed by
Covid-19, some adjustments were made to the sampling and how pilots were conducted,
we outline more important ones below.

First, the selection criteria were meant to match the general population distribution by
age, gender and general education groups, however in Spain, the distribution by education
levels  was  a  concern  as  the  lower  education  target  number  was  not  reached.  Eventually,
42% of participants had university education or equivalent, 39% upper secondary or col-
lege  (non-university)  education  and  19%  primary  or  lower  secondary  education.  On  the
other  hand,  in  Latvia,  there  was  a  slightly  larger  proportion  of  females  resulting  in  53%
females in the final sample and 47% males. Regarding the age, the participants in the final
sample were spread across the range: with 52% in the “active working age” (aged 25–54),
28% aged 55–65 and 20% aged 16–24. The perceived initial digital skill level was fairly
evenly  spread  with  a  slight  majority  (42%)  who  fell  into  the  “intermediate”  level,  30%
at basic and 28% at the advanced level. Secondly, the pilot in Spain was delivered exclu-
sively online, whereas in Latvia it was both in-person and in online mode. The pilots were
financed by the Joint Research Centre and carried out by local partners.

2.2   Methods

2.2.1   Dimensionality analysis

Figure 2 showcases six different models illustrating how digital competence, based on the
DigCompSat  assessment  items  with  attributes  of  knowledge,  skills  and  attitudes,  can  be
conceptualized and measured. First three models refer to multidimensional models:

1.  Multidimensional Competences: This model proposes that competences, depicted as
’a1’ through ’a5’, are distinct dimensions. Each of these dimensions has its respective
items (denoted by boxes) that contribute uniquely to it.

2.  Multidimensional Attributes: This model segments digital competence into three distinct
attributes: Knowledge (K), Skills (S), and Attitudes (A). Each element has its specific
items that directly relate to it.

3.  Multidimensional Combined: A more comprehensive model, this combines the struc-

The multidimensional models posit that five distinct competence areas, each represent-
ing a different facet of digital competence,  are  not  isolated  but  rather  interrelated. These
models assume that while each competence area can be measured individually, they are not
entirely  independent  from  each  other.  Instead,  they  share  varying  degrees  of  correlation,
suggesting  that  an  individual’s  proficiency  in  one  area  competence  may  influence,  or  be
influenced by, their aptitude in another. The crux of these models lies in acknowledging the
interconnected  nature  of  competences  without  asserting  a  single  underlying  general  fac-
tor. Each competence area retains its unique identity, but their correlated nature signifies
the intertwined fabric of digital competence, emphasizing that expertise in one might be
linked to capabilities in another. This perspective offers a nuanced understanding of digital
competence, viewing it as a constellation of related abilities rather than compartmentalized
competences or an overarching general factor (Bollen, 2006; Brown, 2015; Kline, 2015).

In Fig. 2, the remaining three models are bi-factor modes. The bi-factor model consid-
ers the interconnected but independent role of specific competences and a common general
factor of digital competence (G factor). It implies that an underlying G factor shapes gen-
eral digital competence but also that specific competence areas contribute to within-indi-
vidual differences in the general digital competence (Gustafsson & Balke, 1993; Gibbons
& Hedeker, 1992; Gibbons & Hedeker, 1992).

1.  Bi-factor Competences: This model introduces a general factor ’G’, symbolizing the
overall digital competence. While each item (denoted by boxes) relates to this general
factor, they also have specific affiliations with competences (’a1’ through ’a5’).

2.  Bi-factor Attributes: Similar to the previous bi-factor model, there’s a general factor
’G’. However, in this setup, items are related to the general factor and one of the three
elements (K, S, A) simultaneously.

3.  Bi-factor Combined: Merging the concepts of the previous two bi-factor models, this
representation suggests that items are linked with the general factor ’G’, specific com-
petences, and also the three core attributes of digital competence (K, S, A).

Depending on the model that aligns best with the data, the process for scale reduction
should  be  adapted  accordingly.  For  example,  if  the  first  model  of  the  Multidimensional
ones presents the most accurate fit, it would be prudent to construct five separated, stream-
lined scales, each centered on distinct competences within the five DigComp areas. Con-
versely, should the second model, emphasizing the elements of knowledge, skills and atti-
tudes, resonate best with the data, the ideal methodology would involve the formulation of
three individual scales evaluating Attitudes (A), Knowledge (K), and Skills (S). However, if
the third model, encapsulating both above-mentioned models, proves superior in represent-
ing the data, this would advocate for the possibility of devising 5 succinct scales that cater
to the dimension of competences as well as the specific nature of A, K, or S. For the latter
three models, which are bi-factor in nature, their superior alignment with the data would
suggest the potential to propose a singular, overarching index for digital competence. This
index’s construction, however, would hinge upon the subdivision of competences into five
competence areas (as per the fourth model), the categorization into elements of A, K, and

533

S (as seen in the fifth model), or potentially an amalgamation of both divisions (as hinted
by the sixth model). Such a structured approach ensures that any derived scales maintain
fidelity to the intrinsic subtleties of digital competence, as mirrored by the most congruent
model.

In  the  selection  of  items,  we  use  models  for  categorical  data.  For  our  dimensionality
analysis,  however,  some  simplifications  were  necessary.  Our  limited  number  of  respond-
ents restricted our ability to perform Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) for categorical
data.  This  is  because,  except  for  the  first  second  model,  subsequent  models  would  have
more  parameters  than  observations,  leading  to  overfitting  and  unreliable  results.  Conse-
quently,  we  assumed  continuous  indicators,  a  decision  that  effectively  reduced  the  num-
ber of parameters in our models. While this approach is not without its limitations, it is a
common practice in practical settings. Moreover, some simulation studies suggest that such
an approach can yield reasonably good approximations, despite the underlying categorical
nature of the data (DiStefano, 2002). This pragmatic compromise allows us to conduct a
meaningful analysis within the constraints of our sample size, while also highlighting an
area for potential future research with larger datasets.

In our analysis, we utilize recognized fit measures (Hu & Bentler, 1999). We employed
the  Comparative  Fit  Index  (CFI)  and  Tucker-Lewis  Index  (TLI),  values  of  both  indi-
ces close to 1 suggesting ideal fit. For “good fit”, values exceed 0.90, and for “excellent”
they surpass 0.95 (Hu & Bentler, 1998). The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation
(RMSEA) is another fit measure used in CFA models. Favorable RMSEA values are below
0.08, with 0.08–0.1 being marginal, and above 0.1 deemed poor (Fabrigar et al., 1999). We
also consider the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), where values below
0.08 denote good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Lastly, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) assess model quality, with lower values indicat-
ing a more parsimonious fit.

2.2.2   Shortening the scales

The  selection  process  of  the  items  was  based  on  three  premises.  First,  the  final  outcome
must  respect  construct  coverage,  that  is,  chosen  items  will  reflect  the  overall  DigComp-
Sat structure. Second, selected items will maximize the information content and reliability
of  the  shortened  scales.  Third,  selected  items  will  be  measurement  invariant  across  dif-
ferent  groups  of  respondents  including  language  groups,  gender  and  age  for  legitimate
comparison.

Shortening self-report scales is essential in social research to minimize respondent burden
while maintaining psychometric integrity (Stanton et al., 2002a, 2002b). Item Response The-
ory (IRT) has become a widely adopted methodology for achieving this goal across various
disciplines (Goetz et al., 2013). To bring some examples; Edelen and Reeve (2007) utilized
IRT to refine a 19-item Feelings Scale for depression into a 10-item short form. Haroz et al.
(2020)  applied  IRT  to  streamline  pediatric  PTSD  and  depression  scales.  They  reduced  the
original 17-item depression scale to seven items and the 30-item scale to 10 items, maintain-
ing reliability and validity for practical field use. Xia et al. (2019) developed a shortened ver-
sion of the EORTC QLQ-BR23 for breast cancer survivors. Chiesi et al. (2018) shortened the
18-item Need for Cognition Scale to a 10-item version. Datta and Roy (2018) addressed the
challenge of assessing the home environment by shortening the 100-item Home Environment
Inventory using a polytomous IRT approach. Toker and Gültaş (2019) focused on the STEM

Interests Scale from 15 to 12 items.

In  this  paper,  we  also  rely  on  Item  Response  Theory  (IRT)  modeling  for  item  selection
procedures (Bjorner et al., 2004; Ebesutani et al., 2012; Lozano et al., 2018; Schönbrodt et al.,
2012).  This  approach  focuses  on  the  response  to  the  item  and  its  contribution  to  the  meas-
urement. For the purpose of this study, we use the generalized partial credit model, GPCM,
(Muraki,  1992)  which  is  able  to  model  multiple-choice  items  and  use  complete  informa-
tion  about  all  used  categories.  IRT  is  a  better  option  than  Classical  Test  Theory  (CTT)  for
the selection of the items given it offers a more precise estimation of the measured trait by
weighting item influence on the scale by the optimal weighting of the response categories and
items (DeMars, 2010). IRT methodology also provides a wider range of psychometric tools
that  enable  accurate  assessment  of  item  performance,  especially  for  assessing  measurement
invariance (Pokropek et al., 2019).

The procedure of item selection is simple. Item properties are examined to identify misfit-
ting items and items that have relatively low discrimination power. To detect misfitting items
we use the root mean square deviation (RMSD) statistic. The RMSD is a standardized index
of the discrepancy between the observed item characteristic curves (ICC) and the model-based
ICC. This statistic is sensitive to measuring the deviation of the observed item characteristics
from  the  estimated  ICC  both  in  terms  of  slope  and  location  of  the  item  response  function.
Poorly fitting item characteristic curves were revealed with an RMSD smaller than the 0.1 cri-
teria (a value of 0 indicates no discrepancy; in other words, a perfect fit of the model). Addi-
tionally, all items with discrimination power (measured by the a parameter of GPCM) lower
than 0.6 were also excluded.

Measurement invariance was also tested (sometimes referred to as Differential Item Func-
tioning—DIF testing) to assure that the provided tool meets the necessary conditions for valid
group comparisons (Davidov et al., 2014; Meuleman et al., 2023). We wanted to ensure that
the measurement instrument functions in the same way in different groups. That is, respond-
ents with the same value of measured construct receive the same score regardless of a group
membership.  In  this  application,  we  check  measurement  invariance  between:  (1)  countries,
(2)  gender  and  (3)  age  groups.  For  assessing  measurement  invariance,  we  estimated  multi-
group IRT GPCM and investigated RMSD measures for each item and each response category
(see OECD,  2017; Buchholz & Hartig,  2019 for a similar approach). We consider items as
non-invariant with RMSE > 0.3. For assessing quality of items we also used an IECV index.
According to Stucky et al. (2013), IECV “provides the extent to which an item’s responses
are  accounted  for  by  variation  on  the  latent  general  dimension  alone,  and  thus  acts  as  an
assessment of unidimensionality at the individual item level.” (p. 51) As the main aim of this
exercise is to construct short unidimensional scales, items with IECV smaller than 0.5 were
flagged as problematic.

Among  items  that  show  no  psychometric  issues  we  performed  selection  based  on  infor-
mation criteria according to target allocation of items. Item Information Functions (IIF) were
used to show what contribution to the measured trait is provided by each item. This informa-
tion is the main criterion for selecting items. Simply put, for a shorter version of the instru-
ment, we select items that maximize information about the instrument in the range of theta
between -3 and 3.

2.2.3   Internal structure and reliability

For each shortened scale and full-scale, reliability indices are reported. Reported measures
give information about internal consistency which indicates how closely items are related
as a group. We report three indices including the popular Cronbach’s α coefficient which
assumes  an  equivalent  contribution  of  each  item  to  the  total  score  (tau,  τ,  equivalence).
Consequently,  the  α  coefficient  is  considered  as  a  lower  bound  statistic.  Because  of  this
limitation we are also reporting McDonald’s omega.

2.2.4   Validity

To ensure the validity of our proposed shorter versions of the DigCompSat tool, a series
of regression analyses was conducted. In these analyses, we regressed the total score, com-
posed of all items, on several demographic variables (gender, age, education level). Addi-
tionally, we conducted models that include external measures of self-reported digital com-
petence as predictors.

The purpose of these analyses is to determine whether the relationships between these
demographic variables and digital competence, as measured by the original DigCompSat
tool, remain consistent even after the scales have been shortened. If the process of short-
ening  the  scales  has  been  conducted  properly,  it  is  expected  that  one  could  find  that  the
relationships between the demographic variables and digital competence are very similar
to those found with the original, longer version of the tool. In other words, the shortened
versions should maintain the validity of the original tool, accurately capturing the essential
aspects of digital competence as defined by the DigComp framework.

3.3   Reliability

The reliability analysis of the measurement tool across its full, mini and nano versions is
presented  in  Table  10.  The  full  version  (82  items)  exhibits  exceptionally  high  reliability,
with both Cronbach’s Alpha and McDonald’s Omega approaching 0.99. While this near-
perfect reliability might initially seem ideal, it warrants careful scrutiny. High reliability,
especially close to 1, can sometimes be less desirable in psychometric contexts, as it may
suggest redundancy among items (Boyle 1991). This implies that multiple items might be
excessively  similar  or  repetitively  querying  the  same  aspect  of  the  construct,  potentially
inflating reliability scores. Consequently, although the full version provides comprehensive
information across each area of competence, the large number of items may be more than

539

necessary for a single score on digital competences. Therefore, for applications requiring a
single score, the mini or, in some situations, the nano version could be more appropriate,
balancing brevity with sufficient reliability.

The mini-21 and nano-6 versions exhibit lower—but still high—reliability scores, which
is  a  common  occurrence  in  shorter  scales  due  to  reduced  item  variety.  The  mini  version
shows  acceptable  reliability,  with  Alpha  and  Omega  ranging  from  0.75  to  0.88  and  0.76
to 0.90, respectively, across different areas. However, the "Information and Data Literacy"
area in the mini version stands out with the lowest reliability, indicating potential inconsist-
encies in item quality or relevance within this domain. The nano version, while not broken
down by area, still maintains good reliability (Alpha = 0.87, Omega = 0.92), considering its
brevity. This version could be suitable for contexts where a quick, yet reasonably reliable
assessment is needed.

4   Discussion and conclusions

The growing importance of digital competence across various aspects of life, from educa-
tion and work to social interaction and leisure, underscores the need for effective assess-
ment tools. Existing tools, however, can sometimes be too long or complex. In this paper,
we  address  this  challenge  by  proposing  two  shorter  scales  that  capture  the  multifaceted
nature of digital competence – encompassing knowledge, skills and attitudes, while retain-
ing the breadth of competences as defined by the DigComp framework.

To  address  the  limitations  of  lengthy  assessments,  two  shorter  versions  of  the  Dig-
CompSat  scales  address  the  fatigue  and  potential  bias  caused  by  overly  complex  assess-
ments, particularly in populations with lower skill levels. The first version (mini-21) func-
tions as a diagnostic tool enabling a swift assessment of overall digital competence across
all 21 DigComp competences. This is particularly valuable for situations where covering
all 82 DigCompSat items would be overly demanding or time-consuming, such as initial
screenings  for  digital  training.  As  the  instrument  uses  a  4-point  Likert  scale,  scores  per
each competence can be used to determine the level of training; those scoring “0” would
be offered very basic training, whereas, from 1 onwards, the focus would be on guiding the
user to the next level to gain more autonomy on increasingly complex digital tasks.

Websites currently implementing the full DigCompSat (outlined above) would also be
good  use  cases  for  incorporating  mini-21,  it  could  serve  as  a  first  step  to  create  a  more
adaptive  assessment  based  on  the  initial  score  per  competence,  e.g.  if  the  score  is  more
than 0 or a basic threshold, offer more KSA items per competence to get a full picture of
possible gaps.

The second version (nano-6) is a more targeted assessment focusing on the 5 main Dig-
Comp areas. This shorter version can be integrated into broader surveys or used to diag-
nose individuals with lower skill sets. The criteria of scoring 1 or more in all areas could
be used to gauge a basic level of skills, following the similar criteria of the Digital Skills
Indicator (Vuorikari et al., 2022a).

To summarize the relevance of other main areas of this research, our study also empha-
sizes  the  enhanced  fit  of  the  models  integrating  Knowledge,  Skills,  and  Attitudes  (KSA)
and encompassing all competences or competence areas. This result aligns well with the
theoretical multifaceted nature of digital competence and also gives empirical support to it.
Therefore, it supports a key point: measurement tools should incorporate a comprehensive
blend of  both broad competences and core  attributes  like KSA to  ensure a thorough and
representative evaluation. In this way, our proposal of tools is based on a robust framework
and offers a more complete solution compared to other studies focused on the reduction of

541

DigComp  items  without  considering  the  multifaceted  nature  of  digital  competence  (e.g.
Mieg  et  al.,  2024;  Schwarz  et  al.,  2024).  The  results  presented  here  also  carry  ramifica-
tions for digital competence development in general, as this complex nature should also be
reflected in the syllabus for education and training. For this purpose, DigComp 2.2 could
become  a  useful  source  with  more  than  250  examples  of  knowledge,  skills  and  attitudes
(e.g. for training focusing on citizens interacting with AI systems).

Secondly,  the  evolving  landscape  of  digital  competence  assessment  can  benefit  from
shorter  versions  like  the  mini-21  and  nano-6  variations,  the  flexibility  of  these  versions
allows for quick and focused assessments tailored to specific contexts and needs, all while
maintaining the core associations observed in DigCompSat full-82 and its robust psycho-
metric  properties.  This  highlights  an  important  strength  of  our  research,  which  presents
solid evidence for the validity and reliability of the two proposed instruments, exceeding
the capabilities of many existing DigComp developments (Mattar et al., 2022).

Last,  further  strengthening  the  study’s  relevance,  the  two  short  versions  of  the  Dig-
CompSat tool adhere to the Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp). This
alignment ensures the findings are not only theoretically sound but also directly applicable
to  the  current  educational  and  policy-making  landscape  across  Europe  and  beyond.  Dig-
Comp has become integral in shaping digital competence and literacy standards, influenc-
ing curriculum design, instruction, assessment, and policy development in many national
and international contexts, e.g. UNESCO, (Laanpere, 2019; Law, 2018), European Com-
mission’s Digital Skills Indicator (Vuorikari et al., 2022a).

4.1   Limitations of this study

There are also some limitations to this study. First, the developed shorter versions, while
demonstrating validity and reliability, rely on self-reported data. This approach introduces
potential subjective bias and doesn’t directly assess practical skill application. Section 1.2
discusses other limitations too, but also methods to mitigate them that have arisen thanks
to  generations  of  research  knowledge.  Despite  these  potential  drawbacks,  self-reporting
offers practicality, cost-effectiveness, and ease of use for large-scale studies. It allows for
the quick gathering of data from diverse populations.

There  is  also  room  for  highlighting  the  pedagogical  aspects  of  self-reflection  helping
learners  understand  their  knowledge  and  skills  gaps  which  can  potentially  lead  to  active
learning. However, for end-users, this approach might not be as familiar as a typical test-
taking situation and there might be cultural differences in how people behave in such situ-
ations. For digital competence assessment, however, self-reflection can also offer valuable
information about perceptions and attitudes – key components that are difficult to measure
in an objective way. Additionally, this nuanced approach to evaluating attitudes is vital in
understanding digital competence from all its aspects.

Second, shortening the tool enhances efficiency and user-friendliness but can sacrifice
information depth – a common challenge noted by Stanton et al., (2002a, 2002b). While
our  proposal  has  made  all  the  efforts  to  maintain  reliability  and  validity  as  advised  by
Kolva et al (2017) and Reeve and Fayers (2005), it is important to recognize that any pro-
cess  of  scale  reduction  may  affect  certain  facets  of  the  tool’s  comprehensive  scope  and
depth.  There  is  an  underlying  presumption  that  these  abbreviated  versions  will  demon-
strate  psychometric  properties  like  those  of  the  full-scale  tool  when  used  independently.

the Mini and Nano versions might not perform identically to the full-scale tool when used

Recognizing these limitations is important for advancing digital competence assessment
tools. However, it’s equally important to acknowledge the strengths of the proposed shorter
versions. Future research, particularly efforts gathering broader and more diverse data, can
provide a clearer understanding of these challenges and foster their effective management

only test but also expand upon our findings, enriching the understanding and application of
the instruments in the broader context of digital competence.
