Using  microdata  from  the  2023  ENEMDU  survey  and  its  ICT  module,  this  article  focuses  on  the  patterns  of  access,
connectivity, and digital skills among Generation Z in Ecuador using weighted estimations which are consistent with the
complex sampling design of the survey. This analysis of first- and second-level digital inequalities by gender, age, and
geographic area targets individuals aged 15 to 26. These results show a high technological penetration in this age group,
where 74% have a smartphone and 89% of respondent’s report having been online recently, with no differences found for
gender. Age is positively associated with the odds of acquiring a smartphone according to weighted logistic regression
models, whereas most recent internet usage follows saturation patterns that preclude statistically significant differences by
age or gender. Notwithstanding this homogeneity, there appear to be structural differences in this pattern: rural areas are
more dependent on public access points and less digitally literate, with specific differences in digital skills, with women
showing higher operating abilities and men showing some marginal superiority in technical skills. Overall, digital inclusion
in Ecuador’s Generation Z is wide but imperfectly inclusive. Empowering functional digital competencies and minimizing
gaps based on territory and skills are the recommendations. The study acknowledges the limitations of the cross-sectional
design and suggests that longitudinal and multidimensional approaches are needed in future research.

Keywords:

Digital divide, Generation Z, Digital skills, Territorial inequality, digital inclusion.

RESUMEN

Utilizando microdatos de la encuesta ENEMDU 2023 y su módulo de TIC, este artículo se centra en los patrones de ac-
ceso, conectividad y habilidades digitales entre la Generación Z en Ecuador, utilizando estimaciones ponderadas que
son consistentes con el complejo diseño de muestreo de la encuesta. Este análisis de las desigualdades digitales de
primer y segundo nivel por género, edad y zona geográfica se centra en personas de entre 15 y 26 años. Los resultados
muestran una alta penetración tecnológica en este grupo de edad, en el que el 74 % tiene un teléfono inteligente y el 89
% de los encuestados afirma haber estado conectado a Internet recientemente, sin que se hayan encontrado diferen-
cias por género. La edad se asocia positivamente con las probabilidades de adquirir un teléfono inteligente según los
modelos de regresión logística ponderados, mientras que el uso más reciente de Internet sigue patrones de saturación
que impiden diferencias estadísticamente significativas por edad o género. A pesar de esta homogeneidad, parece ha-
ber diferencias estructurales en este patrón: las zonas rurales dependen más de los puntos de acceso públicos y tienen
menos conocimientos digitales, con diferencias específicas en las competencias digitales, ya que las mujeres muestran
mayores habilidades operativas y los hombres muestran una superioridad marginal en las habilidades técnicas. En gene-
ral, la inclusión digital en la Generación Z de Ecuador es amplia, pero imperfectamente inclusiva. Las recomendaciones
son potenciar las competencias digitales funcionales y minimizar las brechas basadas en el territorio y las habilidades. El
estudio reconoce las limitaciones del diseño transversal y sugiere que en futuras investigaciones se necesitan enfoques
longitudinales y multidimensionales.

Palabras clave:

Brecha digital, Generación Z, Competencias digitales, Desigualdad territorial, inclusión digital.

Generation Z, also referred to as centennials, is defined as
people born between 1994 and 2012, who were exposed
to digital technologies and the web early on. Rather than
having to adjust to the prevalence of digital technologies,
Generation  Z  grew  up  in  a  completely  digital  culture  in
which the web and digital environments extend and defi-
ne their lives (Guo et al., 2023). Having never experienced
a world without the internet during their lives, Generation Z
members interact, learn, and view the world and the peo-
ple around them accordingly (Liang et al., 2022).

Generation Z is derived from a world that has undergone
significant social, economic, and cultural transformation:
generations  from  these  decades  have  a  different  pers-
pective on social, cultural, and economic processes that
have  transformed  relationships  between  young  people
and the world (Yasa et al., 2022). Having developed in a
time of capitalist globalization, this generation is raised in
a competitive context that is very immediate, one in which
young people have been reared in a world in which com-
petition and immediacy have a direct and intense influen-
ce on youth attitudes, values, and behaviors as they con-
sume and relate at an interpersonal level (Huang, 2023).
They tend to be irreverent teens who have been born into
continuous change, growing up and have been raised in
ever-changing cultural conditions with inclusiveness and
diversity as cherished norms (Nguyen Ngoc et al., 2022).
As such, some refer to their close relationship with tech-
nology as “digital natives,” posing challenges to conven-
tional models of education and work, as well as requiring
a reconfiguration of social and labor structures to accom-
modate their worldview (Indiahono, 2022).

Generation Z looks remarkably different than previous ge-
nerations in many ways. Its interaction with technology is
not just present, but in many areas of life. Technology is
not  simply  present  but  rather  it  is  the  very  core  of  their
existence,  enabling  high  adaptability  and  a  continual
search  for  innovation  (Graczyk-Kucharska  &  Erickson,
2020). They have unlimited, live accessibility to informa-
tion, which has developed in them the capacity to process
multiple data sources simultaneously, though with a more
fragmented focus (Irwin et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023).
However, the effect of Internet use is not uniform; studies
show that the correlation between usage  and  subjective
well-being is heterogeneous and depends significantly on
the proficiency of the user (Yan et al., 2023).

 Indeed, this culture nurtured the development of a prag-
matic and utilitarian disposition, where agility and efficien-
cy are key values (Silva & Carvalho, 2021). And to this co-
hort, globalization is not just a product of globalization, but
an internalized cultural phenomenon. Generation Z values
diversity and inclusion and actively participates in social
issues,  ranging  from  gender  equality  to  LGBTQ+  rights

(Atkins, 2020). In addition, this involvement is relevant to
collaborative economies and the development of shared
consumption  and  cooperative  economies  that  promote
sustainable consumption habits that extend beyond indi-
vidual interests (Nguyen Ngoc et al., 2022).

Centennials  in  workplaces  often  avoid  rigidly  hierarchi-
cal structures and prefer flexible arrangements that allow
them  to  balance  personal  and  professional  life  (Lukić-
Nikolić  &  Lazarević,  2022).  This  new  cohort  is  hungry
for  a  job  that  they  can  have  purpose  and  purpose  they
value  —  hence  an  increasingly  demanding  labour  mar-
ket  that  redefines  retention  models  (Yilmaz  et  al.,  2024).
Essentially, Generation Z is the departure from yesterday’s
old  paradigm;  the  new  age  born  of  digitalization,  diver-
sity  and  flexibility  pillars  (Jayatissa,  2023).  In  this  study,
Generation Z is defined in conceptual terms as those born
between 1994 and 2012. But for the sake of ease of use
and taking into account the age structure of the ENEMDU
survey data, the age analysis is limited to youth from the
age group of 15 to 26. It represents the essential subset of
the study populations who achieved maturity and are able
sufficiently to engage actively with formal education, the
labor  market,  and  autonomous  digital  consumer  space,
facilitating a closer attention to connectivity behaviors and
functional digital proficiency.

The study led by Vilanova (2019) studies how the young
generation Z children, born from 1994 onward (Spanish:
Generation Z), have become digital natives from the point
of early age fully immersed in a digital ecosystem. Such
people not only adopted new technologies for communi-
cation and entertainment, but also created new modes of
learning and socialization that allows them to understand
and learn information with great flexibility and adaptability
in  the  face  of  large  volumes  of  information.  Additionally,
the study reinforces that Generation Z have been able to
integrate  themselves  into  integrated  multicultural,  global
work environments showing that in such an interconnec-
ted world, younger generations have adapted to a better
way with the arrival of technologies and more technologi-
cal adaptation to life. Within the Latin American context,
Martínez  et  al.’s  study  (2023)  discusses  the  digital  con-
sumption  habits  of  Generation  Z’s  in  Chile,  Colombia,
Mexico,  and  Panama  and  presents  a  comparative  con-
text  on  the  same.  While  cultural  and  economic  realities
vary from country to country, one thing that connects all
members of Generation Z is their deep enthusiasm for di-
gital technologies and strong interest in them. As per the
authors, one of the most important factors in purchasing
behavior is the social network, where a great prevalence
in mobile phones is the key for how the active role of this
generation is played in the digital economy. In conclusion,
for all minor regional differences the connectivity and te-
chnical tendencies appear to be almost constant among
the Gen Z youth of these countries and it is observed that

In Ecuador, Generation Z is heavily using ICTs, especia-
lly in terms of internet and mobile device access. Raised
in  such  a  highly  digitalized  environment,  this  group  has
a  relationship  with  technology  that  is  not  quite  like  that
of  many  of  its  predecessors.  For  every  aspect  of  daily
life,  such  as  information  searches,  social  media  interac-
tion,  and  content  consumption  on  streaming  platforms
(Caicedo et al., 2021) they interact on the internet. Their
digital behaviors are determined by their access to mobile
devices and the prevalence of internet connectivity in their
daily lives (Luque & Herrero, 2019). Time spent connec-
ted to the internet is impacted by socioeconomic status,
technological  devices,  and  the  prevailing  digital  culture
during this generation. Furthermore, data on youth usage
patterns  is  also  related  to  gender,  age,  and  educational
or  work  status  (Elizalde  &  Rodríguez,  2021).  Put  simply,
university  students  and  young  workers  often  devote  ad-
ditional  time  to  online  activity  and  study  due  diligence.
The  preferences  also  reflect  on  the  use  of  devices,  and
smartphones are used for nearly all digital functions, un-
like users of personal computer technologies who come
from earlier generations (Cueva et al., 2021).

In  terms  of  the  different  patterns  of  internet  usage,
Generation  Z  has  common  behaviors  with  other  age
groups in accessing social network sites and information
seeking (Elizalde & Rodríguez, 2021), but their interaction
with technology is more profound and also involves imme-
diacy as well as multitasking. This shows  an adjustment
to  a  constantly  evolving  digital  landscape  in  which  ma-
naging multiple information streams at once has become
an important skill. Their use of smartphones for entertain-
ment, communication, and education shows how integra-
ted technology has become in their daily lives (Caicedo
et al., 2021).

Gender, age, and academic or employment status are in-
teracting with digital behavior in an intersecting and often
diverse  way.  Previous  research  suggests  that  women  in
Generation Z use social media more frequently for social
interaction,  while  men  are  more  likely  to  use  technology

for  entertainment  (e.g.,  video  games)  (Luque  &  Herrero,
2019).  The  academic  or  employment  status  affects  the
time spent online and the type of internet usage as well;
persons who are engaged in academic or work environ-
ments use the internet mainly to seek information related
to their study or professional work (Elizalde & Rodríguez,
2021).

In  the  digital  era  today,  Generation  Z  is  a  demographic
group which displays specific characteristics concerning
its contact with technology and digital behavior (Caicedo
et al., 2021). This study examines the relationship between
demographic  variables—such  as  age  and  gender—and
the  connectivity  patterns  of  young  Ecuadorians  belon-
ging  to  this  generation.  Although  studies  cover  internet
usage within different populations on a large scale, there
is  a  glaring  gap  in  research  on  Generation  Z  within  the
Ecuadorian context (Cueva et al., 2021). This research fills
this gap by employing a descriptive and inferential analy-
sis of a sample of 7,086 participants, examining how they
have been interacting with this digital community in a new
and  exploratory  manner,  through  identifying  correlations
and  common  patterns  with  the  present  study’s  findings.
The  results  offer  useful  insights  for  the  design  of  public
policies and business approaches targeting this genera-
tion, as well as insight into the digital dynamics in develo-
ping societies (Luque & Herrero, 2019).

Cramér’s V were used as alternative measures of relative
association  among  categorical  variables  to  support  the
descriptive analysis with inferential evidence. Also weigh-
ted logistic regression models were estimated to assess
the probability of having a smartphone and being on the
internet recently.

These  models  incorporated  the  survey  design  elements
and  aided  in  identifying  the  impact  of  demographic  va-
riables, including age, gender, and area of residence, on
digital  access  and  use.  Data  estimation  was  performed
using variance corrections for complex surveys and sta-
tistical validation of coefficient estimates and significance
tests.  Statistical  calculations  were  generally  conducted
using  R  based  on  specialized  packages  for  large  and
complex  types  of  surveys,  together  with  weighted  infe-
rential  model  estimations.  The  initial  exploratory  actions
and  basic  consistency  checking  were  carried  out  on
SPSS  (version  24.0)  only.  All  final  estimation,  along  with
all descriptive and inferential processing, was done enti-
rely in R, thereby allowing for rigorous characterization of
Generation Z digital inclusion and accurate assessment of
inequalities related to digital access and skills.

The  null  hypotheses  formulated  for  this  study  are  as
follows:

H1: There are no significant differences in the place of in-
ternet use between urban and rural areas.

H2: There are no significant differences in the frequency of
internet use between men and women.

H3: There  are  no  significant  differences  in  daily  internet
connection hours across demographic groups.

H4: There are no significant differences in the pri-
mary online activity between men and women.

Table 3 shows the distribution of the main place of internet
connection among Ecuadorians belonging to Generation
Z,  by  the  geographic  area  of  residence.  The  data  show
statistically  significant  urban  and  rural  differences  (p  <

home is the most common facility (80%), people connect

quaintances’  homes  (5.9%).  These  results  suggest  that
while the home is a primary location where young people
mainly  access  the  internet,  structural  disparities  remain
between urban and rural areas. But rural youth, in particu-
lar, have depended more on common or community-rela-
ted spaces for connection, implying uneven infrastructure
and access of individuals via the network.

Volumen 4 | Número 1 | Enero - Abril - 2026CONCLUSIONS

This study describes the patterns of access, connectivity,
and  digital  competencies  of  Generation  Z  in  Ecuador—
defined as persons aged 15 to 26—based on microdata
provided from the 2023 ENEMDU survey and weighted es-
timation models adjusted for the complex survey design.
The results verify a high percentage of digital penetration
in this group of individuals: 74% owners of a smartphone,
89% recent Internet use, and no statistically significant di-
fferences exist between the genders. Yet, while this seems
to be the case, the logistic models provide subtlety to this
seeming homogeneity. Age was not found to be a signifi-
cant predictor of Internet use (p = 0.416), indicating that
usage is almost universal; while, on the positive and sta-
tistically significant side, age has an association with the
chance of having a smartphone (p < 0.001).

This  fact  shows  that,  even  within  a  general  adoption
context,  there  remains  a  material  access  gap  for  youn-
ger  groups.  No  relationship  was  reported  between  the
genders in dimensions of second-level use. Internet use
frequency  is  the  same  between  men  and  women  (p  =
0.4022),  is  generally  employed  on  a  daily  basis  and  the
most  popular  activity  is  digital  communication,  with  no
difference  (p  =  NA).  However,  two  structural  inequalities
appear that break through even usage of uniformity. First
of all, a long-lasting geographic inequity is identified: you-
th originating in rural areas tend to reach for public spaces
to  facilitate  connection  more  heavily  (9.8%  compared  to
1.5%), and also they are more likely to be digitally illiterate
(12%  compared  to  2%)  because  they  have  poor  infras-
tructure, lack of technology and digital capital. Secondly,
while there is no difference in usage frequency or the type
of use, there are marked differences in digital skills; wo-
men  have  higher  proficiency  in  simple  operational  tasks
(e.g.,  copying  files;  p  =  0.030)  whereas  men  are  higher
in  technical  work  (software  installation  4.0%  vs.  2.3%;  p
= 0.036).

Overall, the findings indicate that digital inclusion among
Generation Z in Ecuador is broad but not fully equitable.
Overcoming  access  gaps  does  not,  in  itself,  ensure  the
homogeneous  development  of  digital  competencies.
Consequently,  public  policies  should  move  toward  stra-
tegies  aimed  at  strengthening  functional  and  technical
skills,  as  well  as  reducing  territorial  disparities  that  sha-
pe  the  meaningful  appropriation  of  digital  technologies
among youth.
