Digital inclusion has become a priority to achieve a more effective integration of people in the constant trans-
formation processes, in a world dominated by technology. This makes digital literacy a fundamental tool in the
process of digital inclusion, which also leads to relevant social changes. This represents a challenge for Latin
American countries, which, in many aspects, are behind in the use of technology compared to countries in more
advanced regions such as North America, Europe, and Asia. The objective of this research has been to conduct an
analysis based on a systematic literature review to understand the current trends of inclusion and digital literacy
in Latin America, from the perspective of its importance in society. The study focused on the principles of the
Prisma 2020 declaration and used ScienceDirect and SciELO as search engines on a sample of articles published
between 2020 and 2025, and taking into consideration specific inclusion criteria, which led to the selection of 21
articles. The results indicated that the most studied topics were digital inclusion in older adults, corporate digital
inclusion, digital inclusion in university education, digital inclusion of women, digital inclusion in rural contexts,
digital inclusion in social contexts, and digital inclusion of indigenous peoples. In conclusion, it was corroborated
that there is a digital divide in the region, which requires concrete public policies to minimize it, and whose
influence affects the socio-economic conditions of the most vulnerable populations.

1. Introduction

Digital inclusion refers to the acquisition and development of tech-
nological skills, as well as the use of the Internet. Other definitions are
involved in its conception, such as acquisition, which is related to the
availability of the internet, digital devices, and technological tools, and
skills, which are related to the ability to use effectively these tools and
technologies. Usage refers to the degree to which people actually engage
with digital platforms and services (Yan & Xing, 2025).

The appropriation of technology related to social inclusion implies
facilitating  people  and  communities  ’  use  of  technological  tools  and

services  so  that they  can fully  participate  in  society  (Rivera  &  Calva,
2016).  To  achieve  this  digital  inclusion,  variables  such  as  access  to
technology,  the  skill  level  of  community  members,  the  technological
infrastructure available in the territory, the socioeconomic context, the
way technology is perceived within the community, specific needs and
demands, and empowerment over its use must be considered (Arango
et al., 2024).

Although in recent years it has been noted that the number of people
without access to digital technology has been decreasing, there are re-
ports from international agencies that indicate that this situation con-
tinues  to  occur,  even  in  areas  such  as  United  Kingdom,  where  a

were still 5.3 million adults who did not use the internet, as well as 6.4
million adults who, although using the internet, had limited ability to

According to World Bank figures, by 2022, despite the increase in
technology use caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, there were 3 billion
people  without  internet  access,  with  developing  countries  accounting
for 96 % of cases (Puliti, 2022). Similarly, the World Economic Forum’s
report  on  digital  inclusion  by  2025  also  indicates  that  despite  global
broadband internet coverage, 33 % of the world’s population (2.6 billion
people) remains unconnected. This creates a gap between those who do
have access and those who do not, highlighting a digital divide that has
not  been  closed  despite  the  increase  observed  in  the  wake  of  the
COVID-19  pandemic.  This  situation  is  due  to  various  factors  that
constitute barriers to connectivity, some of which include lack of access
to devices and data, lack of digital skills, and limited access to digital
infrastructure (Poggi, 2025).

The lack of access to the Internet has been defined as “digital divide",
a  concept  that  has  gained  relevance  over  time  and  that  has  several
meanings, for example, the difference between people with and without
access  to  new  technologies  (Shin  et  al.,  2021).  Recently,  various  re-
searchers have pointed out that the digital divide should be understood
as a hierarchical issue in which Internet access and skills for its use are
included.  Therefore,  several  factors that  hinder  access  to  the  Internet
and  new  technologies,  such  as  poverty,  illiteracy,  etc.,  into  account
(Tang et al., 2024).

In response to the digital divide, digital literacy has emerged, which
integrates  the skills  needed to read,  write, and  communicate through
technology and digital media (Hafner et al., 2015). It is also related to
how  people  build  relationships,  think,  and  interact  with  digital  tech-
nologies, which in turn significantly increases technological literacy and
improves the ability to access, evaluate, and use information effectively
(Ariya et al., 2025).

At  the  Latin  American  level,  regional  and  national  agendas  have
pointed out the importance of digital inclusion, due to the knowledge of
the  principles  of  inclusion  of  sustainable  development.  It  has  been
recognized  that  there  is  a  digital  divide  that  not only  manifests  itself
outwardly, but also within the countries of the region, where inequality
in access and use of digital technologies is evident. The digital included
and excluded are a factor of both social and political studies (Andonegui
& Samaniego, 2019). Therefore, digital literacy has been a key concept
to reduce the digital divide in the region, especially in the educational
sphere, where the incorporation of technologies has been fundamental
to transform the way of working, coexisting, and accessing information,
which leads to the generation of knowledge (Cabero & Fern´andez, 2018;
George & Avello-Martínez, 2021).

According  to  a  report  by  the  Andean  Development  Corporation
(Italia, 2024), which is part of the Development Bank of Latin America
and the Caribbean, 10 % of households in the region do not have fixed
broadband internet. Furthermore, services such as 4G mobile internet
are  inaccessible  to  20  %  of  the  population,  placing  the  region  at  a
disadvantage compared to the countries that make up the Organization
for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), with a gap of only
7  %.  This  report  also  indicates  that  the  digital  divide  is  accentuated
among people over 65, those with lower incomes, and those living in
rural areas. On the other hand, Rodríguez-Pedr´o et al. (2024) indicate
that  the  digital  divide  in  the  region  is  increased  by  the  presence  of
traditionally marginalized indigenous communities, which do not have
access to the internet and technology. The same is true of remote rural
communities where telecommunications infrastructure is very limited.

Given  the  importance  of  this  topic  not  only  in  Latin  America  but
globally, systematic review works have been carried out that compile
the information obtained in the field of study in different specific areas.
Thus, authors such as George and Avello-Martínez (2021) compiled in-
formation on the conceptualization of digital literacy at the global level.

This was compiled from the Scopus database between 1996 and 2020,
and they concluded that in order to conceptualize digital literacy, it is
necessary  to  consider  and  define  other  terms.  These  are:  information
literacy, computer literacy, and media literacy, since literacy based on
the incorporation of technology is articulated around them, especially
with a focus on education.

Other reviews conducted on the digital inclusion of women obtained
information  from  databases  such  as  Web  of  Science  and  EBSCO  Host
Discovery  Services  between  2012  and  2020  Gupta  and  Kiran  (2025),
Scopus between 1951 and 2021 (Meyerhoff & Erhi, 2022), and Dialnet,
ERIC,  Google  Scholar,  ProQuest,  Scielo,  Scopus,  and  Web  of  Science
between 2017 and 2021 (Pel´aez-S´anchez & Glasserman-Morales, 2023).
These  studies  found  that  lack  of  education  is  a  determining  factor  in
women’s digital inclusion, and although the importance of this issue is
emphasized, most studies have focused on comparing women with men
in terms of access to and use of technology. This situation reflects the
need  to  broaden  the  review  of  the  factors  that  drive  gender  digital
inclusion.

Similarly, reviews of digital inclusion in education have been con-
ducted  based  on  information  obtained  from  Scopus,  EBSCO,  and  Sci-
enceDirect  between  2017  and  2022  (P´erez-Valles  &  Reeves-Huapaya,
2023), based on ACM, IEEE, Scopus, and Web of Science between 2016
and 2024 (Yue, 2024), and through a documentary review with a gen-
eral  search  up  to  2022  (Su,  2023).  These  studies  concluded  that  the
digital divide in education is due to the lack of public policies aimed at
digital literacy. In addition, it was established that innovation in edu-
cation through digitization is essential, especially after the experience of
the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, the need to contextualize a framework
that recognizes both human and non-human agents in digital literacy
education was highlighted, especially in the era of AI, which favors the
improvement of skills in young students.

Another topic studied in systematic reviews was the digital inclusion
of adults. One example is the work carried out by Adams et al. (2025),
who  used  14  databases,  including  the  most  important  ones  such  as
MEDLINE, Scopus, APA PsycInfo, Web of Science, IEEE, and ERIC, for
the period 2014–2023. Similarly, Benavides and Chipana (2021) con-
ducted a systematic review on this same topic, using the Scopus, SciELO,
Redalyc, Dialnet, and Google Scholar databases, limiting the period to
2015–2020. Oh et al. (2021) worked with electronic libraries such as
PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, and MeSH to conduct a systematic review on
the digital inclusion of adults in the period 2009–2020. All these reviews
agreed that digital inclusion and literacy in older adults have strengths
and weaknesses, highlighting the importance of digitally literate older
adults, especially in the use of technologies in the areas of health and
law. The studies recommended implementing public policies that allow
for the inclusion of this age group in the use of new technologies, pro-
moting  discussion  on  the  implications  of  digital  inclusion  for  older
adults, and addressing the barriers that limit their acquisition of digital
skills.

The studies reviewed showed a tendency toward global and topic-
specific  reviews,  with  very  little  contribution  from  studies  conducted
by Latin American authors. Likewise, the topic has been little addressed
in  recent  years,  with  the  most  recent  studies  published  in  2023  and
2024. Given the importance of digital inclusion in a region where the
percentage of people without access to technology exceeds that of other
regions, and given the lack of relevance given to this topic as a subject of
review,  especially  in  the  last  five  years,  and  the  very  specific  topics
addressed, a review was developed that focused specifically on articles
published  by  Latin  American  authors.  This  review  analyzes  their
contribution  from  different  thematic  contexts  through  a  descriptive
mapping  of  the  information.  It  also  attempts  to  answer  the  following
research  question:  What  are  the  characteristics  and  contributions  of
research on digital inclusion and digital literacy in Latin America over
the last five years?

Based on this question, the following objective was established: to
analyze the characteristics and contributions of research conducted in

2. Methods

A methodology was applied based on the guidelines established in
the  PRISMA  statement,  which  standardizes  the  procedures  for  devel-
oping systematic reviews and meta-analyses. This minimizes the risks of
bias  and  ensures  the  selection  of  a  representative  sample  to  provide
meaningful results (Page et al., 2021). The process consisted of a series
of  sequential  steps,  for  example,  identification  of  relevant  studies,
mapping of information, analysis of information, and communication of
results (Peters et al., 2020).

2.1. Application of the prisma declaration

As a first step, the search engines ScienceDirect (Scopus) and SciELO
were  established.  In  the  case  of  Latin  America,  researchers  tend  to
publish mainly in Scopus and SciELO, as these databases are the most
highly regarded in the researcher classification systems in the region (in
some countries, only Scopus is accepted). Using the ScienceDirect search
engine (which only generates Scopus records) and SciELO is considered
representative,  without  detracting from other regional  and global  da-
tabases,  which  could  be  used  in  future  research.  This  study  period
(2020–2025) is considered relevant because no reviews covering these
years were found, which led to an update of the topic. Furthermore, the
databases  used  are  the  most  relevant  within  Latin  American  research
classification systems, so the main research by authors from the region is
indexed  in  them.  For  the  search  in  English,  the  keywords  used  were
“Digital inclusion" AND “Digital literacy" AND Latin America". For the
Spanish  search,  the  keywords  used  were  “Inclusi´on  digital"  AND
“Alfabetizaci´on  digital"  AND  “Latinoam´erica".  In  this  way,  the  search
was covered in the two main languages of publication in the region and
in the two main databases used. Following the protocol established in
the  Prisma  statement,  went  on  to  define  the  inclusion  and  exclusion
criteria, which are detailed in Table 1.

Fig. 2 shows the distribution of articles by year of publication. Most

Analysis  of  the  country  of  origin  of  the  articles  revealed  that  the

Peru) contributed the same number of articles, with the same number of

A total of nine specific topics were identified that guided the studies

knowledge.

Regarding the distribution of the articles by language of publication,
it was observed that 12 of them were published in Spanish (57.14 %) and
9  were  published  in  English  (42.86  %).  Regarding  the  research

3.2. Analysis of specific topics

3.2.1. Digital inclusion in elderly people

The digital inclusion of the elderly is a topic of great importance, as it
was analyzed in 31.82 % of the articles reviewed. As shown in Table 3,
five countries conducted research on the digital inclusion of the elderly,
with emphasis on qualitative research (6), which indicates a tendency
towards  a  perceptual  and  opinion-based  analysis.  In  addition,  the
problem ofwas analyzed the digital divide was analyzed, i.e., the dif-
ference between the digital inclusion of the elderly and that of young
people,  which  leads  to  the  need  to  propose  government  policies  that
reduce  this  gap  and  enhance  the  participation  and  knowledge  of  the
elderly in technology. Another important aspect is digital competencies
in  education.  Older  teachers  have  more  difficulties  in  adapting  to
technological  changes  and,  therefore,  their  digital  inclusion  is  mini-
mized, which generates stress due to deficiencies in the use of technol-
ogy; this is known as “technostress".

3.2.2. Business digital inclusion

Digital  inclusion  at  the  business  level  was  the  subject  of  study  in
13.64  %  of  the  articles  reviewed,  the  details  of  which  are  shown  in
Table  4.  Two  of  the  three  articles  focused  on  studies  conducted  with
samples  from  several  countries  in  the  region,  and  one  specifically  in
Ecuador. All the studies were developed under quantitative research, as
they are focused on business sciences, which are quantitative in nature.
In  general,  they  highlight  the  importance  of  digital  skills  training for
employees,  i.e.,  enhancing  their  digital  inclusion,  which  in  turn  is
related to improving business operations, technological skills, and so-
cioeconomic conditions.

3.2.4. Digital literacy in rural contexts

This topic was addressed in 9.09 % of the articles reviewed (Table 6).
The  two  countries  that  addressed  this  specific  topic  were  Chile  and
Mexico,  whose  studies  were  based  on  a  quantitative  methodology.
Among the contributions, it notes that in both countries there are de-
ficiencies in terms of literacy and digital inclusion of people living in
rural areas and emphasizes the need to increase Internet penetration and
ICT training to reduce the digital divide with respect to people living in
urban areas.

3.2.5. Digital inclusion of women

Two articles focused on the issue of women’s digital inclusion were
obtained (Table 7). The articles were developed by authors from Brazil
and  Colombia  under  quantitative  approaches.  Both  articles  show  the
inequality of women’s access to technology and, therefore, low digital
inclusion, which is common in both countries. This not only affects the
level  of  digital  skills  of  women,  but  also  their  competitiveness  when
entering the labor market, so that inequalities must be addressed with
the seriousness they deserve.

3.2.6. Digital inclusion from a social perspective

From the social perspective, two studies were conducted focusing on
how  digital  inclusion  relates  to  people’s  social  environment  and  its
improvement (Table 8). The studies were conducted in Colombia and
Mexico, using qualitative and quantitative methodologies. Both studies
highlight the inequality in access to technology, which limits the digital
inclusion  of  certain  vulnerable  groups,  such  as  women,  indigenous
people, and the elderly. Also highlights the role of socio-cultural con-
ditions in the learning of technologies, so that these groups should be
taken into account in digital literacy plans, based on the characteristics
of their social environment, in order not only to improve their digital
inclusion, but also their socioeconomic conditions.

Due to the importance of digital inclusion, research in this field has
been conducted worldwide in recent years, with Latin America being a
region that is no exception to this reality. Although Latin America has
presented diverse research approaches in this field, the digital inclusion
of the elderly has been the most relevant, especially in countries such as
Argentina,  Brazil,  Chile,  Mexico,  and  Peru,  which  coincides  with  the
global trend.

Most of the papers deal with specific points, such as the digital divide
that  affects  the  elderly,  and  the  lack  of  concrete  policies  by  the  gov-
ernments of the region to address the digital divide. The results about
the digital inclusion of older adults are consistent with the findings of
Adams et al. (2025). These authors, in their review on the digital in-
clusion of homeless older adults, maintain that few digital interventions
have  been carried out for this age group, although they indicate that
these are acceptable and feasible to apply. For their part, Benavides and
Chipana (2021) agreed that although it is evident that the importance of
digital  inclusion of the elderly to achieve their access to justice, they
observe deficiencies of the State in the implementation of public policies
that allow their inclusion in the use of new technologies. In turn, Oh
et al. (2021) reviewed the methods for measuring digital literacy aimed
at older people and found that various instruments with strengths and
weaknesses  are  used,  generating  diverse  results.  Consequently,  they
concluded that studies conducted in this thematic field should review
the measurement instruments to ensure their suitability and that they
adequately reflect the problem.

This specific issue has also been addressed from the perspective of
various international organizations, which have highlighted the prob-
lem not only in Latin America but also in a global context. The Economic
Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC, 2021) pro-
duced a report indicating the gap in access to and use of digital tools
between young people and older adults. In this report, they indicated
that, in the case of Europe, only 25 % of older people have basic digital
skills, a low figure compared to other age groups, where digital skills
range from 50 % to over 80 %.

Consistent with the findings of this review, ECLAC and other orga-
nizations  such  as  the  Organization  for  Economic  Cooperation  and
Development (OECD) emphasize the need to reduce the digital divide
among older adults through policies that enable them to participate fully
in the digital age and access its benefits. This involves democratizing
access to technology, offering tailored training programs, ensuring the
accessibility of devices and infrastructure, and guaranteeing a safe and
user-friendly digital environment, with the ultimate goal of reducing the
digital divide and strengthening a more equitable society (OECD, 2022).
In this same context, Mohan et al. (2024) mention the existence of
three  main  factors  that  influence  the  digital  divide  observed  in  older
adults: socioeconomic status, health problems, and age-related limita-
tions. According to the authors cited, this is independent of the country’s
income level, which would imply that it is not the economic conditions
of  countries  that  limit  the  digital  inclusion  of  older  adults  in  Latin
America, but rather the sociodemographic and health conditions of in-
dividuals. Yang et al. (2025), for their part, agree with the other studies
analyzed  in  stating  that  the  digital  inclusion  of  older  adults  is  condi-
tioned by the policies that governments adopt to promote the distribu-
tion of digital infrastructure, the digital literacy of these individuals, and

the adaptation of digital devices to the conditions of older adults.

Another relevant aspect detected in the review was business digital
inclusion,  which  highlights  the  importance  of  digital  literacy  and  in-
clusion of employees, which, in turn, is related to the improvement of
business operations, technological skills, and socioeconomic conditions.
Review  works  such  as  Calderon-Monge  and  Ribeiro-Soriano  (2023)
highlight  the  importance  of  business  digitization  and  indicate  that  it
affects all areas of companies, including employees’  digital skills and,
therefore, their technical and socioeconomic improvement. In the same
context,  and  in  line  with  the  findings,  Saranya  and  Vasantha  (2023)
concluded that, despite the importance of digital inclusion of employees
in companies, in some companies, there is still inadequate management
of knowledge, training, resources, and a lack of awareness about it.

CAF  (2024)  points  out  that  in  Latin  America,  despite  widespread
internet use, there is still a digital divide in companies, as only 55 % of
them have a website, a low figure compared to OECD countries, which
report  77  %.  This  data  is  consistent  with  the  findings  of  the  review,
which  highlights  the  importance  of  inclusion  and  digital  literacy  in
businesses,  but  which  has  not  yet  been  addressed  efficiently
(Gonz´alez-Tamayo  et  al.,  2023;  Guti´errez  et  al.,  2025;  Ochoa  &
Coello-Montecel, 2023). The report also highlights a wider gap in the
more advanced use of technology, such as the use of data management
centers.

Another  thematic  category  addressed  in  the  articles  reviewed  was
digital inclusion in universities. S was concluded that it is necessary to
include the use of technological tools in the curricula, as they are a weak
point in several Latin American countries, since they limit the acquisi-
tion of digital competencies, digital inclusion, and the improvement of
graduates’ placement in the labor market, which improves their socio-
economic  conditions.  Previous  reviews,  such  as  P´erez-Valles  and
Reeves-Huapaya (2023), highlight the importance of digital inclusion in
education;  however,  they  point  out  the  lack  of  public  policies  that
improve  students’  digital  competencies,  especially  in  the  use  of  ICT,
which is consistent with what was obtained. For their part, Jim´enez et al.
(2023) emphasize the importance of teachers’ digital literacy and their
competencies in  pedagogical technological  knowledge. In their litera-
ture review, they conclude that the continuous incorporation of tech-
nological  tools  in  education  is  important  to  achieve  teachers’  digital
literacy and enable them to face the challenges of the times related to the
practice of learning models in the educational context.

Yue (2024) reinforces this point by highlighting the importance of
digital  literacy  in  the  field  of  artificial  intelligence,  which,  taken
together, is consistent with the findings of this review. This is due to the
importance  being  given  to  AI  in  education,  and  therefore,  digital  in-
clusion in this field is linked to the use and mastery of AI tools applied to
education. The author cited refers to digital inclusion as a fundamental
part of AI literacy and emphasizes the need to address such literacy in a
transdisciplinary  and  interdisciplinary  manner,  covering  both  techno-
logical and social aspects. To achieve this, a new conceptual framework
is needed, one that includes both human and non-human agents linked
to AI literacy education for young students. This process must begin with
the digital literacy and inclusion of the student population.

In rural contexts, studies were identified in which digital inclusion
was the subject of research in countries such as Chile and Mexico, where
the findings emphasize that the literacy and digital inclusion of people
living in rural areas are deficient, so greater Internet penetration and ICT
training  are  needed  to  achieve  a  reduction  in  the  digital  divide  with
respect  to  people  living  in  urban  areas  (Basoalto-Rojas  et  al.,  2022;
Martínez-Domínguez  &  Mora-Rivera  (2020)).  It  is  clear  that  in  Latin
America there is a deficiency in terms of digital inclusion of people living
in rural areas, which represent a significant percentage of the popula-
tion: in 202,0 it was estimated to be 18.9 % (Saravia-Matus & Aguirre
H¨ormann,  2019).  This  problem  is  due  to  the  low  implementation  of
territorial  planning,  which  is  necessary  for  sustainable  development
based on environmental protection and the well-being and quality of life
of the collectives (Casas-Matiz et al., 2023). Authors such as Okocha and

Correa  and  Pavez  (2016) indicated  that  among  the  causes  of  the

which  shapes  people’s  personalities  and  attitudes  toward  the  use  of

means  that  isolated  rural  communities  face  specific  challenges  in
adapting  to  digital  technologies,  which  is  a  challenge  that  must  be
considered in decision-making and public policy formulation.

Another  study  consistent  with  this  review  was  conducted  by
Nohuddin et al. (2025) en Malasia, who proposed promoting community
participation, especially in hard-to-reach areas, to reduce the existing
digital inclusion gap between urban and rural communities. The authors
agree  with  other  studies  in  indicating  that  policy  reforms  must  be
implemented to ensure sustainable digital growth in rural environments.
Their main contribution is that communities should promote initiatives
that enable an inclusive digital ecosystem, with the full participation of
residents. In this regard, education is also essential to closing the digital
divide by promoting digital literacy. To this end, infrastructure must be
improved in order to achieve socioeconomic development and improve
the quality of life in rural areas.

In this same context, Mwansa et al. (2025) conducted research in a
rural area of South Africa, the results of which highlighted significant
obstacles to digital inclusion. These obstacles were infrastructure, cost,
and  digital  literacy,  which  impede  access  to  and  use  of  technology.
Given  these  systemic  barriers,  specific  interventions  are  required  to
develop digital opportunities and achieve inclusive rural development.
This  is  also  consistent  with  what  has  been  observed  in  rural  areas  of
Latin America.

The digital inclusion of women has also been identified as one of the
specific issues that has gained special relevance in recent years. In Latin
America,  mainly  in  Brazil  and  Colombia,  original  research  has  been
conducted on this issue and has concluded that women have less access
to digital literacy, which generates inequality in access to technology
and,  therefore,  low  digital  inclusion  in  women.  The  lower  digital  in-
clusion of women in Latin America affects their level of digital skills and
their competitiveness to integrate into the labor market. Among previ-
ous reviews on this topic, Gupta and Kiran (2025) found that the low
inclusion of women in technology is conditioned by education, since in
countries  or  regions  where  women  have  less  access  to  education
compared to men, there is greater digital exclusion and greater depen-
dence of women on men. For their part, Meyerhoff and Erhi (2022) point
out, after their review, that despite the relevance of the topic, its study
has  been  deficient  and  that,  in  many  cases,  the  importance  of  digital
inclusion of women and girls is recognized, but without going deeper
into  the  subject.  In  addition,  they  identified  research  biases  towards
countries  with  large  economies  and  little  geographic  segmentation  in
the study, which coincides with the fact that only two articles have been
collected  in  Latin  America  on  this  topic.  This  was  also  observed  by
Pel´aez-S´anchez  and  Glasserman-Morales  (2023),  who  stated  that,  ac-
cording  to  their  review,  there  is  limited  international  interest  in  the
study  of  women  in  digital  inclusion,  with  a  low  incidence  of  public
policies focused on reducing the digital divide between women and men,
which is consistent with what has been observed in Latin America.

In line with observations in Latin America, Richardson and Wilson’s
(2024) work in Africa, specifically in Zimbabwe, showed that the gender
digital  divide  affecting  women  with  lower  technological  skills  has  a
cultural  and  emotional  component.  In  another  paper,  Galpaya  &  Zai-
nudeen, 2022 noted that progress has been made in some parts of South

Asia in narrowing the gender digital divide, however, in India and Sri
Lanka, two key barriers to women’s meaningful access to digital inclu-
sion were identified. These barriers specifically relate to digital knowl-
edge/awareness and skills, and it was also found that in these countries,
levels  of  digital  skills  and  awareness  of  digital  opportunities  are
particularly low among women. This component must be addressed so
that the learning and digital literacy process is interesting and attractive
to  women,  serving  as  motivation  for  the  development  of  their  digital
skills. For their part, Sowmya and Pai (2025), in a documentary review,
indicated that the digital divide not only affects the inclusion of women
from a social perspective but also from an economic one, limiting their
ability to function in business. Barriers such as digital illiteracy, socio-
cultural restrictions, cybersecurity risks, and inadequate technological
infrastructure were also identified, all of which are consistent with what
was observed in Latin America.

The document published by the World Bank (Aranda-Jan and Qasim,
2024)  highlights  that  obstacles  to  women’s  digital  inclusion  persist
globally, mainly due to inequalities in accessibility, digital connections,
technological infrastructure, low levels of education in certain regions,
restrictive gender and social norms, and concerns about online safety
and security. All these factors indicate a connection between the various
emerging issues observed, which converge in gender inequality in in-
clusion, not only in Latin America but globally.

Digital inclusion and digital literacy are also recurring themes in the
articles reviewed from a social point of view. However, their study has
been limited, with articles published by researchers from Colombia and
Mexico, highlighting the scarce digital inclusion of groups considered
vulnerable, such as women, indigenous people, and the elderly, which is
not only conditioned by state policies, but also by sociocultural condi-
tions related to learning technologies. Therefore, it is recommended that
these groups be addressed in digital literacy plans, taking into account
their sociocultural characteristics to improve their digital inclusion and
socioeconomic  conditions.  The  observed  coincides  with  what  Ramír-
ez-Casta˜neda and Sepúlveda-L´opez (2018) raise, who, in their review on
the subject, emphasize how reduced is the research and the formulation
and  implementation  of  public  policies  in  the  technology  access  gap,
without taking into account the characteristics of the people who are
part of the communities, ignoring that each one has a different access to
technological  products  and,  therefore,  the  functionality  that  they  de-
mand  and  obtain  is  different.  For  their  part,  Md  Sabri  et  al.  (2023)
showed that, although there is an increase in the use of ICTs to address
digital inequalities, in some parts of the world, there are people with
little  access  to  technologies,  which  limits  their  digital  inclusion  and
enjoyment of their benefits, which is linked to the socioeconomic and
cultural conditions of the people.

As a global issue, social inequality is a determining factor in digital
inclusion. This factor widens the gap in many countries, including Latin
America, due to the social inequalities that exist in the region, as indi-
cated by reports from various organizations such as the Inter-American
Development Bank (IDB, 2024) and CAF (De La Mata & Berniell, 2022).
These organizations report that nearly 77 % of wealth is concentrated in
the richest 10 % of countries, a situation that is causing growing concern
about the digital inclusion of socially vulnerable groups. Individuals in
these  disadvantaged  groups  may  have  difficulty  accessing  and  using
digital technologies, which in turn is a direct consequence of state social
policies.  These  policies  should  be  geared  toward  digital  literacy  pro-
grams that reduce not only the digital divide but also social inequalities
(M´endez-Domínguez et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2022).

In this social context, it is important to mention indigenous peoples,
who have a clear digital divide, which limits their digital inclusion and,
therefore, their access to knowledge, to the Internet, and other techno-
logical tools. This problem has been a problem for a long time, as Cat-
al´an  (2010) already  highlighted in  a review, with  the existing gap in
indigenous peoples with respect to access to technology, which limits
the potential social development of the communities. This is consistent
with the findings, since, although digital inclusion in indigenous peoples

was considered a category in itself, it continues to be an issue of broad

In this context, Soto-Hern´andez et al. (2020), in their study on the

years, established that no significant progress has been made and that,
despite the Mexican state’s efforts in digital literacy, these have not been
sufficient. This situation compromises the education and development of
indigenous communities in the face of the challenges of the digital age.
Other authors who also highlight the existence of a digital divide among
indigenous  peoples  in  Mexico  are  Martínez  Domínguez  and  G´omez

involved in the design and implementation of digital policies. All this

It is worth highlighting that the problems regarding digital inclusion
in the region, which had been evident for several years, became more
pronounced  as  a  result  of  the  COVID-19  pandemic.  This  situation
highlighted the technological deficiencies and the digital divide in the
region, which considerably affected certain vulnerable populations that
are less likely to adapt to technology. This is the case of older adults, for
whom  there  was  no  digital  literacy  plan  in  the  region  compared  to
younger adults (Oddone & Pochintesta, 2021). Similarly, Beltr´an (2023)
addressed  this  issue  and  indicated  that  one  of  the  main  factors  that
widened  the  digital  divide  was  the  increased  use  of  devices  and  be-
haviors regarding them, due to the fact that inappropriate use of these
devices  led  to  digital  inequalities,  beyond  access  to  technology  or
availability of time.

Similarly, the point made by Lopez-Ercilla et al. (2021) stands out, as
they also mention that the COVID-19 pandemic was a turning point in
which deep gaps became evident. These are linked to the use of digital
technologies,  not  only  among  older  adults  but  also  among  other
vulnerable groups such as women and indigenous peoples. To this end,
they  highlighted  the  importance  of  this  knowledge and  the  establish-
ment of mechanisms to bridge the digital divide through literacy cam-
paigns focused on these vulnerable groups.

This  leads  to  a  reflection  on  the  need  for  digital  inclusion  for  the
development  of  society,  which  became  evident  after  the  pandemic.
Warschauer (2003) had already indicated this when he mentioned that
this aspect must be addressed by effectively integrating technology into
communities,  institutions,  and  societies.  They  emphasize  that  the
importance  lies  not  in  the  physical  availability  of  equipment  and
internet access, but in people’s ability to use technologies and partici-
pate in meaningful social practices. This was highlighted by van Deursen
and van Dijk (2018) and has increased in recent years with the rise of
emerging technologies such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), which, since
its  emergence  and  deployment  across  various  social  dimensions,  was
already  seen  as  a  technology  that  would  deepen  existing  digital  in-
equalities in the region (Lombana, 2018).

Subsequently, Lugo and Barrera Rojas (2024) reflected on the chal-
lenges and characteristics of AI in the region, arguing that it generates a
more collaborative technology and encourages not only people’s access
to and use of technologies, but also meaningful appropriation capabil-
ities. This, in turn, is closely linked to the need for them to access basic
digital knowledge, allowing them to address digital literacy in light of
new technological realities.

5. Emerging trends

It was found that research on digital inclusion in the region has been
approached from various angles, but gaps remain that need to be studied
in depth, such as internet access, technological infrastructure, and lit-
eracy policies. Research is needed on people’s perceptions of this issue,

especially in rural areas and among vulnerable groups. Another aspect
that  should  be  considered  in  studies  is  inclusion  and  literacy  in  new
technologies,  such  as  artificial  intelligence.  All  of  this  is  necessary  to
understand how the new digital and technological revolution is being
addressed in  the region. Similarly, it  would be interesting  to conduct
more detailed quantitative (meta-analysis) and bibliometric studies to
expand knowledge on the subject.

6. Conclusions

Digital  inclusion  and  literacy  in  Latin  America  have  increased  in
recent years, following the global trend. However, the review of scien-
tific papers reveals that there is still a digital divide that has not been
able  to  be  reduced,  mainly  due  to  the  lack  of  public  policies  that
encourage  the  greater  presence  of  technological  advances,  especially
regarding the use of the Internet, ICTs, and digital tools applied to ed-
ucation. This has had a significant social impact, which has been positive
in part, but has also generated inequalities among vulnerable groups of
the population.

The main issue identified is the digital inclusion of the elderly, with a
greater number of studies conducted in which there is coincidence in
stating that, despite advances, the penetration of technology in this age
group  has  been  partially  compromised  by  limited  knowledge  and,
mainly, by the scarcity of policies aimed at serving the elderly popula-
tion  and  making  them  digitally  literate,  which  harms  their  socioeco-
nomic situation.

Other relevant issues were also identified, such as digital inclusion in
rural  environments,  in  education,  among  women,  in  society,  and  in
indigenous populations. In all these areas, it was observed that positive
efforts are being made to achieve greater digital inclusion of the popu-
lation;  however,  much  remains  to  be  done,  since  in  Latin  America  a
greater governmental commitment is needed to meet the needs of access
to technology of the various social groups, which would imply a sub-
stantial improvement in the socioeconomic conditions of the population,
taking into account the socio-cultural conditions of the different human
groups.

7. Limitations

In addition  to the  limitation of  the study  period to five  years, the
number of countries included was low in relation to the total number of
Latin American countries, since digital inclusion or digital literacy has
not been investigated in all countries, and, in some cases, the regions
where it has been investigated did not fit the established period or did
not meet any of the other inclusion criteria. Another limitation was the
use  of  only  two  databases  (Scopus/ScienceDirect  and  SciELO),  which
excluded articles available in other databases such as Web of Science,
Redalyc,  and  Latindex  from  the  analysis,  which  could  have  further
enriched  the  results  obtained.  Furthermore,  only  open-access  articles
were used, and restricted-access articles that could have also enriched
the topic and discussion were discarded. All of these aspects should be
taken into account in future reviews.
