1. Introduction

In this digital age, our information-oriented society accentuates modern and changing technologies.
Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) have introduced a whole range of new services
to individuals, businesses, and governments in terms of modern technological progression, econ-
omic growth, improving welfare services, and even how politics are conducted (Cruz-Jesus et al.,
2016; Zhao et al., 2007). ICT is considered a skilled category; therefore, deﬁciency of ICT skills can
create discrimination among people in certain societies (Matli & Ngoepe, 2020). Digital competency
and mobile learning (m-learning) are conceptualized as the most signiﬁcant factor for digital social
inclusion and realization of citizens’ formation towards a modern knowledge society. Citizens from
low-income and rural households tend to have less access to digital technologies, which results in
poor ICT skills concerning the use of digital technologies. There exists a prominent role of ICT and
its signiﬁcant advantages for the development of human societies both technologically and econ-
omically (Alzahrani et al., 2018; Khasawneh & Abu-Shanab, 2013; Samoilenko & Osei-Bryson, 2018;
Weerakkody et al., 2016; Ziemba, 2019). Despite various signiﬁcant advantages of ICT in public
sector services, citizens still have problems using e-government services (United Nations, 2018),
which shows a negative correlation between digital use and social exclusion. One of the key

explores the following key research questions:

RQ1: How does ICT-enhanced learning promote the immersion and development of citizens’ digital competency
about e-government services?

RQ2: How do e-government ICT training programs help to increase digital competency and reduce the digital
divide in a developing society?

RQ3: How do ICT training programs mediate the relationship between digital inclusion and citizens’ use of e-gov-
ernment services?

2. Literature study

Evidence on the eﬀectiveness of ICT training programs in promoting the adoption of e-government
services is limited. The emergent availability of electronic public services does not mean that they are
being used by every level of society. Rather, they are not being used by vulnerable clusters of people
within the society, and this can arise due to low ICT literacy rate, diﬃculties in navigation, and digital
divide discrimination (Gauld et al., 2010; Ma & Zheng, 2018, 2019; Moon, 2002; Zolotov et al., 2018).

by the government through reducing the cost of service, and improved acceptability by citizens
(Cumbie & Kar, 2016) to use ICT as a societal development tool.

2.1. Technological innovations for modern information societies

In the contextual development of information societies, governments have to adopt numerous pol-
icies for both the supply and demand sides of IT services (Raman & Yap, 1996), which may include
modern ICT infrastructures, policy formation, and training on ICT services. ICT has a tremendous
impact on human development and helped in building information societies with economic stability
(Jurado-González & Gómez-Barroso, 2016). Technological innovation is an important dimension for/
of governments (Kottemann & Boyer-Wright, 2010), and e-government innovations are being intro-
duced daily by many countries (Mehr, 2017). The use of mobile technologies in e-government
systems plays a prominent role in anchoring e-participation strategies to improve a country’s
social sustainability (Ochara & Mawela, 2015). Various research has shown that e-government ser-
vices are mostly used by young, highly educated citizens with high-income levels (Gauld et al.,
2010; Rosenberg, 2019; Woolley & Peterson, 2012), but it is being used by every segment of
society (United Nations, 2014, 2016, 2018). Governments around the globe are now focusing on
the more innovative and holistic tasks of service innovations in the public sector value delivery
process. Open government is one tool used in the open society concept, with a major aim of provid-
ing citizens access to government data, activities, and proceedings (Kassen, 2019). In recent years
this mechanism has expanded to create opportunities for citizen participation (Hu et al., 2019)
and collaboration in uplifting/improving society with the help of modern ICT infrastructures.

Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) has enormous potential for the development of smart societies. It can
provide innovative advances in areas such as healthcare, agriculture, education, and transportation.
AI in e-government services can improve the government’s capability to make data more accessible
and help citizens navigate government services more intelligently (IBM, 2018). The big data frame-
works are used in e-government service formation in the context of digital public services and policy
formalization (Giest, 2017; Pencheva et al., 2020). One of the productive advantages of big data is
that it can improve the prediction of various policies deﬁned by the government regarding their
future outcomes (Cook, 2014). Many studies discuss the characteristics of smart cities (Allam &
Dhunny, 2019; Buuse & Kolk, 2019; John, 2015; Stratigea et al., 2015). In a smart city concept,
diﬀerent electronic data collected via the Internet of Things (IoT) is used to transform information
that is further used for eﬃcient resource utilization by the government. This reﬂects the deployment
of ICT-enabled approaches and solutions that are needed/required to solve the urban sustainability
issues around the globe. The concept of smart governance focuses on exploring the phenomenon of
smart collaboration (Viale-Pereira et al., 2017), where ICT works as the catalyst between government
agencies and citizens to develop modern information societies.

A digital divide is a social and economic inequality concerning ICT access, usage, and skills within
a country, particularly skills related to internet use, which generates an information gap within the
society (Sun et al., 2015). The digital divide exists in all countries (United Nations, 2018) that needs to
be bridged. Previously the digital divide was only related to lack of access to the internet and con-
necting devices, but this problem has been marginally solved with the help of geographical technol-
ogy advancement. Currently, we are facing the new digital divide regarding the speed and quality of
hardware devices along with necessary procedures to use those devices, which is described as mul-
tiple digital divides (United Nations, 2018). Furthermore, the term digital divide is further extended
to the concept of infrastructure divides, gender divide, and connectivity divide. Access to technology
is the key factor for successful services delivery and this can be achieved by introducing and devel-
oping various distributions channels in developing countries where the digital divide is high (Joshi &
Islam, 2018). In the e-government context intermediaries can be possibly used to help to bridge the
gap in internet connectivity especially in developing countries where internet access is limited (Sein
& Furuholt, 2012). In Brazil, community technology centers (CTC’s) are bridging the gap through pro-
viding mobile phones and computers accessibility to those face digital inequalities in sociotechnical
experience (Nemer, 2018).

2.4. Emerging mobile learning paradigm and ICT access in developing countries

Mobile technology has changed our world in ways formerly unimaginable. Mobile devices permeate
our daily lives, providing unparalleled access to communication and information. There are approxi-
mately 3.5 billion active mobile phone users around the world (Statista, 2020), which highlights the
power, functionality, and aﬀordability of these devices. According to a survey in eleven emerging
and developing countries across four global regions, a median of 53% across these nations now
has access to a smartphone capable of accessing the internet and running apps (Silver et al.,
2019). Mobile usage is helping poverty-stricken societies to create more jobs, increase the levels
of literacy, and bridge the gap in easy access to quality healthcare, education, and economic devel-
opment. Therefore, m-learning can support e-government training in new ways due to its ﬂexibility,
accessibility, and access to online sources (Han & Shin, 2016). Concerning the global development in
mobile technologies speciﬁcally in developing countries, the establishment of such technologies has
enhanced the ability of citizens to voice their demands and place increasing pressure on govern-
ments to respond to those demands. Mobile networks can deliver services to citizens, creating an
opportunity for governments to reach more citizens (Raja et al., 2012).

The m-learning paradigm is signiﬁcantly aligned with adequate ICT access in developing
countries. The poorer the ICT access environment, the lower the opportunity to exploit such services
in a country (Alderete, 2017). Citizens whose mobile devices have internet access can consume e-
government services eﬀectively; therefore, in developing countries, shared public internet access
is an attractive, eﬀective, and low-cost way to share the beneﬁts of digital technology (Girard,
2015). The net beneﬁts of diversiﬁed participation of citizens in e-government services go beyond
providing access to digital technologies rather than helping to develop digital societies.
In a
study by (Alderete, 2017), it was concluded that ICT access and working skills have a positive
impact on the socio-economic development of society, along with an ability to grab digital
access. Civil society organizations can play a key role in supporting citizens to access and use ICT
services by designing appropriate programs together with citizens to overcome barriers of ICT
access in developing countries. Motivated and active Pakistanis have been trained to become life-
long agents of change in their society. These citizens inspire communities to address key issues, fos-
tering a culture of community ownership, and are bringing systemic digital changes in a society. The
Government of Pakistan (GoP) ‘Digital Pakistan-2020’ vision addresses the need to provide internet
access to its citizens and (particularly) strengthen the ICT infrastructure in remote and rural areas
(PMDU, 2019). Such activities are signiﬁcant for continuing to reﬁne ICT development so that the
digital divide does not escalate (McKenna et al., 2017). The digital divide issue also includes the will-
ingness of citizens to use e-government services regardless of the aﬀorded access to service (Matli &
Ngoepe, 2020). M-learning is the emerging trend in Pakistan, and higher education institutions are
also oﬀering mobile education. The key drive for the increase of m-learning growth is linked with the
growth of cellular mobile subscribers; currently Pakistan has 166.47 million cellular mobile and local
loop subscribers with internet penetration rate of 36.18% (PTA, 2019).

research questions to create hypotheses. This section clariﬁes our implicit theory regarding e-gov-

ALT provides generous understanding regarding the learning intention of adults, and help trainers to
be more eﬀective, practical, and responsive to learners’ needs. According to Malcolm Knowles,

3.3. Study hypotheses

This section discusses the development of hypotheses concerning our research questions. In an
information society, public services must be disseminated to all without discrimination (S. K. Kim
et al., 2019), which will positively reinforce digital inclusion. Mobile learning is an eﬀective tool in
such a training environment was noted in studies by (Karmila, 2013; Sampson, 2006), who suggested
that mobile learning is an eﬀective tool for skill training and it is also suitable to deliver personalized
training. The expansion of ICT has added value to technological learning for citizens (Blau & Shamir-
Inbal, 2017, 2020). Digital literacy training is used to increase digital skills and has been recognized as
a key competency that citizens should acquire (Buckingham, 2015; Zhang & Zhu, 2016). ICT plays a
signiﬁcant role in the socio-economic development of a country, including the intensity of ICT usage
and skills (Alderete, 2017; Fasasi & Heukelman, 2017). Citizens’ ICT usage proﬁciencies regarding the
consumption of e-services can create trust in the government services along with user satisfaction
(Santa et al., 2019), and the co-creation of such electronic services is possible due to citizens’ invol-
vement (Hu et al., 2018). ICT can play a major role in the community development process only if the
users (citizens) are empowered with adequate ICT skills (Fasasi & Heukelman, 2017), and this can be
possibly be attained by deploying ICT training programs. ICT training programs are a pathway to
poverty reduction as they help to increase citizens’ quality of life (Alderete, 2017). Training programs
regarding the use of e-government services will reduce the gap between the delivery of e-govern-
ment public services to every citizen in a country and leaving no one behind (United Nations, 2018).

4. Research methodology

To test the hypotheses, the quasi-experimental research method was used to conduct, assess, and
reﬁne the structural relationships in the conceptual framework. Quasi-experimental methods were
developed to help researchers to carry on to experiment under control conditions, represented in
Figure 3. The treatment is not given to participants randomly, rather they are treated as an intact
group. This is commonly used where the experiment group members are used directly as subjects
(Edgar & Manz, 2017). ‘Face Validity’ of the training module was performed to test the content evidence
and to verify that it covers all learning aspects as well as being suitable for e-government ICT training.

4.2. Contextual training modules design

The e-government ICT training modules include theoretical and practical learning related to online
searching of e-government services (SES), application through online government job portal (GJP),
online citizens’ passport services (CPS), Pakistan citizen portal (PCP), online ticket reservation ser-
vices (TRS), land record management system (LRMS), and electronic taxation services (ETS). The
training curriculum was based on the ‘ASSURE’ instructional design model. Course/module trainers
prepared their presentations along with the training material in advance and submitted them to
the training coordinator/authors for quality assurance. Trainees were freshmen in such type of e-
government training programs and gave consent to participate. The seven modules had an
average duration of 92 hours of m-learning. The class was divided into 3 sessions – beginning
(20 days), middle (20 days), and end (30 days). The observations of this course took into
account the methodology of adult learning principles, principles of collaborative learning, and
the principles of interaction.

4.3. Training participants

The experimental sample group consisted of 438 trainees (97.4% of all trainees), of which 191 (43.6%)

5. Data analysis

Researchers use various statistical methods to develop and endorse their research ﬁndings, which
are renowned between two generations of statistical application methods. For this true experiment,
the overall measure of eﬀects from repeated samples is calculated by analyzing the diﬀerence in the
eﬀect after training. In this study, the paired sample t-test was used to determine the mean diﬀer-
ence between the two sets of observations. In this test each entity is measured twice, resulting in
pairs of observation (Fritz & Berger, 2015; C. Kim et al., 2013).

5.1. Factor and reliability analysis

Bartlett’s Test was used to test sample variances equality; small values <0.05 of the signiﬁcance level
indicate that factor analysis is adequate (Snedecor & Cochran, 1989). To measure the sample ade-
quacy, Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin’s (KMO) test with a threshold value not less than 0.70 (Cerny & Kaiser,
1977) was used (see Table 2). The internal consistency was measured using Cronbach’s α, with an
overall value greater than 0.8 representing good internal consistency between measurement
items (McDonald & Ho, 2002; Rosenblad, 2015; Tavakol & Dennick, 2011; Vacha-Haase et al., 2002).
Composite reliability (CR) with a cut-oﬀ value of ≥ 0.70 was used to test the internal consistency
of the items (Urbach & Ahlemann, 2010). The results are tabulated in Table 3.

7. Observations during training

The ability to adapt, use, and access e-government services are critically important to social inclusion.
The citizens’ ability to make use of available computers and the internet to engage in social practices
is what makes the diﬀerence (Warschauer, 2004), therefore, e-government needs to be accepted as a
service. During this study, some e-learning barriers (Assareh & Hosseini Bidokht, 2011) were also
observed, which should be addressed before the start of such training programs. Prior knowledge
of the e-services being oﬀered to the citizens by the government is not properly advertised. Tech-
nological diﬃculties include interface design and non-availability of the national language or multi-
lingual support. Computer anxiety was normally observed among the participants during the
training program. Technophobia was observed as citizens were afraid of using the technology,
especially due to the privacy issues. Citizens do not trust services oﬀered by the government.
Digital divide was clearly an issue – various trainees do not have internet services in their home vil-
lages and could only use e-government services in urban areas. The non-availability of feedback
support in the system creates a lack of value co-creation between the citizens and the government.
Due to the absence of proper IT resources and service delivery failure, citizens lose interest in these e-
services. Administrative support by the technical team is not standardized, which results in a lack of
interest and trust. Motivation to complete e-government training programs depends on the duration
and delivery of professional knowledge to the trainees of such programs.

8. Research implications

This study provides useful insight for both theoretical and practical research regarding e-govern-
ment technological innovations for information societies.

8.1. Theoretical contributions

This study oﬀers signiﬁcant implications for research and theory that contribute to the ICT develop-
ment literature in the context of public sector e-government services. With the intention to
strengthen the digital
inclusion of citizens, e-government ICT training programs must provide
deeper, skill-based, and active learning experiences for citizens to integrate technology in a signiﬁ-
cant way. The results of this study contribute to the body of knowledge in several ways. First, this
study addresses the literature gap by oﬀering valuable insights and extends the ICT skills literature
by empirically examining citizens’ perception of e-government ICT skill training programs. This study
helps us to improve the understanding of interactions between humans and technology in develop-
ing society (Alderete, 2017; Alshawi & Alalwany, 2009). Second, it makes a valuable contribution to

advancing the understanding of citizens’ social and behavioral opinions associated with utilizing e-
government training programs. Thus, it contributes to the concept of citizens as active and compe-

(United Nations, 2018). Third, our ﬁndings reveal that improving citizens’ satisfaction level through
ICT training in the e-government sector can help strengthen digital inclusion in society, whereas

support that citizens’ participation in e-government ICT training programs is positively associated

that highlights that harnessing the beneﬁts of advanced ICTs requires appropriate infrastructure,

grams can promote a positive technological usage impact on society. The digital divide was mini-
mized through the training program, and this study can serve as an initial blueprint for
understanding the eﬀects of ICT training programs on citizens’ behavior within and across societies.

8.2. Practical contributions

In practical terms, the results can contribute to eﬀective ICT instructional design for innovative train-
ing concerning e-government services. Training includes the development of ICT skills in combi-
nation with cognitive-social tasks and explicit guidance to achieve a higher service level in the
consumption of e-government services. In connection with the practical implications, training can
help to improve citizens’ knowledge and skills, which results in the continued use of e-government
services (Bhuasiri et al., 2016). The ﬁndings of this study also have policy implications. First, govern-
ments need to assure citizens of their commitment to reducing the digital divide. Second, govern-
ments need to motivate citizens with certain incentives and design an ICT training program that ﬁts
their general lifestyles so it can be adopted more easily. Third, ICT training is a powerful driver to
increase digital inclusion for developing societies, and the perception of e-government service
value depends signiﬁcantly on how well governments uphold citizens’ service needs. Fourth,
these training programs can be replicated to remote areas that have poor ICT knowledge,
thereby reaching the last mile for a transformative impact across societies. Fifth, this study can
play a critical role in eﬀorts to mitigate the digital divide in Pakistan as well as other developing
nations.

9. Conclusion

Governments around the globe are increasingly adopting various methods to motivate citizens
regarding the use of e-government systems. This study showed that ICT enables public sector
reform through the essential consideration of ICT training as an enabler. The major emphasis of
applying ICT in e-government is to assure the implementation of government policies; moreover,
the decisive goal is to enrich citizens’ trust in government. The literature contains conceptual
work on motivating citizens, but more practical work is required to capitalize on such concepts.
Based upon UN survey reports that show a negative correlation between digital use and social exclu-
sion, we introduced and deployed speciﬁc ICT training programs to improve the success of e-gov-
ernment services with the concept of leaving no one behind. We introduced a proper training
approach to technologically train citizens based on adult learning theories and the ASSURE instruc-
tional model. After training, the trainees were conﬁdent, knowledgeable, cognizant, and empowered
to use e-government services. These training programs can also empower rural communities by pro-
viding life-saving knowledge, water resource management, and, most signiﬁcantly, bridging the

digital divide through ICT inclusion in society. As government businesses successively transfer to
online services, our research ﬁndings speak strongly to the importance of e-government training
programs and methods of ﬂexible training to include all segments of society. Initial studies indicate
that the beneﬁts of m-learning in e-government services are likely to accrue to those governments
that adopt policies and programs that not only encourage technological change but also promote
essential institutional changes and redesign processes. Our study results provide useful evidence for
the governments who are interested in creating and designing the training programs to exploit the
e-government systems that increase the probability of e-government services consumption by the
citizens. The implications of this study are beneﬁcently relevant for the development of society as e-
government is the application to promote/foster digital inclusion in a developing society.

10. Limitations and future work

The research context was hypothetical; therefore, this study has limitations and raises questions for
future research. This study focused on individuals who are primary internet users with knowledge of
e-government systems. However, citizens with limited internet knowledge are also an important
target group for e-government services, and future research can explore their intentions towards
ICT training programs. A key challenge to ICT training programs is to motivate citizens’ participation,
especially those who may lack the resources required to participate, and future research can be
extended to these citizens by enabling them with ICT resources. This unique study in Pakistan
may be regarded as a methodological strength of the study. Future research should be extended
geographically. We also plan to implement this training program within a particular government
department to develop a government viewpoint concerning its employees.
