Objectives: To assess digital health literacy in Brazil using the eHEALS questionnaire and contribute valuable data
to the global understanding of how populations assess and use digital health information to manage their health.
Study design: Cross-sectional survey.
Methods: We administered the eHEALS-Br questionnaire to 401 participants in public high-traffic areas. eHEALS
scores  were  categorized  into  three  levels  -  inadequate,  problematic,  and  sufficient  -  with  educational  adjust-
ments. We used linear regression for data analysis to examine the impact of different sociodemographic aspects
on digital health literacy.
Results: The findings reveal differences across gender, economic status, and educational level, which shows how
vulnerable groups have lower digital health literacy, which can impact their capacity to manage their health and
increase health inequalities.
Conclusions:  A third  of  the  sample  exhibits  low digital  health  literacy,  underscoring  the  need  for  government
interventions and public policies focused on the digital environment to enhance digital health literacy. Adding to
the limited research on digital health literacy in Latin America, our study contributes essential data from the
Global South, informing international strategies to address health disparities worldwide.

1. Introduction

Digital  devices  have  been  incorporated  into  the  day-to-day  life  of
most people, which also plays a role in how the population interacts with
health  services.  Using  digital  sources  to  access  health  information  is
becoming more commonplace, with the growing availability of online
platforms,  apps,  and  content  focused  on  well-being  and  disease  pre-
vention.1,2,3 In this context, digital health literacy has become essential
to  ensure  that  people  can  gather  trustworthy  information  digitally  to
take  informed  decisions  about  their  health.4,5 In  this  research,  we
measure  the  digital health literacy  of Brazilian adults and  investigate
how  it correlates with their sociodemographic characteristics and the
digital platforms they use to gather health information.

In  a  developing  country  such  as  Brazil,  where  socioeconomic  in-
equalities  are  significant,  our  study  on  digital  health  literacy  brings

attention to the impact of said disparities in health literacy, which can be
of  importance  to  other  countries  from  the  Global  South,  which  face
similar challenges.6,7 The digital divide in this context goes beyond ac-
cess to digital devices and internet access but includes the capacity to
assess whether health information available online is trustworthy to be
able  to  understand  how  to  properly  assess  and  manage  one’s  health
effectively.8,9 Understanding  groups  that  face  greater  difficulties  can
help target policies to reduce this digital divide. Through our data and
statistical findings, we are able to provide a diagnosis of digital health
literacy,  which  can  serve  international  organizations  that  aim  to
improve access to health information in different contexts.

digital health literacy as the ability to find, understand, evaluate, and
apply information from digital sources. Digital health literacy is essen-
tial for people to accurately assess the reliability of information found on
the internet, so that they can make informed health choices.20

With  the  COVID-19  pandemic,  the  dissemination  of  health  infor-
mation  on  the  internet  has  expanded,  broadening  the  possibilities  of
access to health related information.21 However, along with this higher
reliance  of  digital  information,  misinformation  became  more  wide-
spread, with some even calling it an ‘infodemic’.22,23,24 This raised the
stakes of low digital health literacy, as it could risk improper care in the
face of these new risks.25,26 Given this recent growth of misinformation,
we  have  opted  to  add  items  on  preferred  digital  health  information
sources, as looking for reputable sources also plays a role in the kind of
health information people have access to Ref. 27.

Digital  health  literacy  plays  an  essential  role  in  promoting  better
health  outcomes  and  public  health,  since  it  measures  the  capacity  of
individuals to adequately assess health information online. This, in turn,
increases preventive health practices since better-informed individuals
are more likely to understand how to properly manage their health.28
Measuring digital health literacy is a first step in creating solutions to
increase it since it allows for more effective targeting of interventions.29
This article aims to measure digital health literacy in Brazil using the
eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS-Br), identifying groups with low, me-
dium,  and  high  literacy  levels.  To  this  end,  we  surveyed  408  partici-
pants. We used convenience sampling and the Brazilian version of the
eHEALS,  translated  and  adapted  by  Mialhe  et  al.,20 which  shows
excellent evidence of validity for measuring the level of digital health
literacy.

Analyzing recent studies, we observed that the digital health literacy
scale  has  proven  to  be  consistent  in  different  countries  and  cultures,
including Jordan,30 Nigeria,31 Saudi Arabia,32 Japan,33 Italy,34 China,35
Turkey,36 South Korea,28 United States,37 Indonesia,38 France,39 Brazil20
and others. The instrument has also been used to measure digital health
literacy in different groups, such as university students,39,40,41–43 health
professionals,35 hospitalized  people,44 young  people,45 people  with
cancer,46 and adults who use the internet as their main source for health
information.47

According to the literature, the main factors that influence the level
of  digital  health  literacy,  in  general,  are  income,32,48,49,50 schooling,
computer  knowledge,31,28,34,32,30,36,42,49,35 and  age.51,32,37,52,48,50 A
study  conducted  in  Nigeria31 shows  that  high  levels  of  digital  health
literacy  are  also  associated  with  a  lower  likelihood  of  anxiety  and
depression.28

Research  carried  out  with  health  professionals  indicates  that  they
present  adequate  digital  health  literacy,  which  facilitates  interaction
with  their  patients.30,35 In  Turkey,  university  students  who  find  the
internet  useful  for  making  health  decisions  have  significantly  higher
digital  health  literacy.36 On  the  other  hand,  in  Jordan,  the  average
digital  health  literacy  score  among  undergraduate  students  is  inade-
quate.53 In relation to groups more prone to inadequate literacy, older
people  with  lower  levels  of  education  and  income  have  lower  digital
health literacy.32,37

In Brazil, the literature related to digital health literacy is focused on
cultural  adaptation  and  validation  of  the  psychometric  properties  of
instruments for measuring digital health literacy.54,55,20,56,57 Analyses
conducted  in  Brazil  reveal  that  sociodemographic  characteristics  are
directly  associated  with  digital  health  literacy.52 Moreover,  the  main
factors that impact literacy generally include gender, age,52 income,49
and education level.55,49

A study carried out with university students in Brazil52 identifies that
higher  literacy  levels  are  associated  with  males  attending  a  public
institution and having people close to them who search for health in-
formation online. However, when the analysis is extended to the general
population in an online survey, it is found that education and income are
the main elements correlated with higher scores,55 while factors such as

age and gender have little or no influence on it.49

Our goal with this article is to assess the level of digital health lit-
eracy in Brazil by using the eHEALS. Specifically, we explore the rela-
tionship between digital health literacy and sociodemographic factors,
as well as the preferred online sources for seeking health information. By
doing so, our study seeks to examine how social inequalities influence
digital health literacy. Understanding these factors is crucial to promote
health equity.

2. Methods

2.1. Design and ethical issues

The  research  protocol  was  approved  by  the  Ethics  Committee  for
Research Involving Human Beings of the Getulio Vargas Foundation -
CEPH (N. P.229.2023). Before the participants answered the question-
naire, the objectives, methods, and procedures of the study were clearly
explained to them, and informed consent was obtained from all partic-
ipants.  Since  no  identifying  information  was  collected,  no  additional
consent was required for the publication of personal data.

We  administered  the  questionnaire  to  401  participants  through
intercept surveys in different high-traffic locations in the Federal Dis-
trict,  such  as  Brasília  Airport,  the  Plano  Piloto  Bus  Station,  shopping
malls, parks, subway stations, universities, UBS (Basic Health Units) and
the  surrounding  area,  to  obtain  the  greatest  possible  diversity  in  our
sample, having representation of different groups of the population. The
survey took place between October and November 2023. It should be
noted that no data identifying the participants was collected in order to
guarantee their privacy and anonymity.

2.2. Instrument

Norman  and  Skinner19 developed  the  eHealth  Literacy  Scale
(eHEALS), an 8-item scale measuring the level of digital health literacy,
which  aims  to  measure  the  self-perception  of  individuals’  abilities  to
search  for,  evaluate,  and  apply  health-related  information.  For  this
study, we used the adapted and validated version of the eHEALS ques-
tionnaire, along with a socioeconomic questionnaire and two questions
about the media used to search for health information online.

The eHEALS has eight statements on a Likert scale, with a 5-point
variation  between  1  (strongly  disagree)  and  5  (strongly  agree),  with
four items on the ability to use the Internet and four items on the use of
health information. The sum obtained is a minimum of 8 points and a
maximum of 40, with higher scores indicating a higher level of digital
health literacy.19

According  to  Mialhe  et  al.,55 we  used  the  classification  in  three
bands, which should be controlled by the level of education, a factor that
significantly influences the scale’s results. In this way, individuals with
up to incomplete elementary school education are analyzed using the
following  classification:  low  (up  to  10),  medium  (11–25)  and  high
(27–40). Individuals with a higher level  of schooling are classified as
having low digital health literacy (up to 25), moderate (25–32) and high
(33–40).

3.2. Structural validity

We ran an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using our dataset. We
found  that  our  data  supports  a  unidimensional  factor,  with  Unico  =
0.977 (above 0.95), ECV = 0.842 (slightly below the threshold of 0.85),
and  MIREAL  = 0.228  (below  0.3  and  confirming  unidimensionality).
The  CFI  = 0.964  (good  fit),  the  TLI  or  NNFI  = 0.95  (good  fit),  the
RMSEA  = 0.08  (not  good  but  within  acceptable  range,  CI:
0.0420–0.0975), and the RMSR = 0.0709 (close to the 0.05 threshold).
The reliability estimates are also reasonable: the McDonald’s Omega =
0.851  (is  good  as  it  is  above  0.80),  the  Cronbach’s  Alpha  = 0.826
(implied acceptable internal consistency), Greatest Lower Bound (GLB)
= 0.914  (suggesting  a  high  reliability),  and  the  Factor  Determinacy
Index (FDI) = 0.923 (above 0.90, implying that factor scores are well-
determined).  The  Kayser-Meyer-Olkin  (KMO)  test  is  0.84  (suggests
that  the  data  is  suitable  for  factor  analysis),  the  Salomon  split-half
method derives a ratio communality index = 0.951 confirming that a
split of the sample derives two subsamples that are equivalent in their
structure, suggesting high representativeness and stability of the results
across different subsamples. Overall, our statistical results suggest that
the  sample  is  a  good  size,  well-behaved,  and,  to  some  extent,
generalizable.58–60,61–63

4. Discussion

Our results show that about one-third of the sample (33.42 %) has a
low digital health literacy (DHL) level, which indicates lower access to
quality  information.  This  makes  this  population  more  exposed  to
misinformation  and  vulnerable  to  inadequate  or  dangerous  health
practices.67,68,69 This  scenario  becomes  even  more  critical  when
considering  that  low  digital  health  literacy  further  exacerbates  in-
equalities, disproportionately affecting already marginalized groups and

In an increasingly digital world, reliance on non-specialized sources
and  a  lack  of  skills  and  knowledge  in  using  digital  tools  can  lead  to
poorer health outcomes and higher long-term costs.73,74 Additionally, it
increases  dependence  on  others  for  health-related  decision-making,75
compromising  autonomy  and  adopting  appropriate  preventive  mea-
sures, further worsening health disparities.76

Access to digital devices and the internet is also challenging in this
context, especially in the Global South, where economic and educational
inequalities further accentuate the digital divide.77,78 This can be seen in
our results, given that those who do not look for health information have
significantly lower scores across all of our models. This might be due to
those people not having access to digital devices, which can inhibit ac-
cess to health information and hinder the navigation of the healthcare
system.79,80 Similar  challenges  have  been  found  in  other  countries  in
Latin  America,  where  the  digital  divide  further  reinforces  health
inequality.81,82

The lower level of eHealth literacy in Brazil, evidenced by an average
eHEALS score of 27.1, reflects a pattern observed in several countries of
the Global South. While in developed nations such as the United States
(30.3)83 and Australia (28.2)84 the scores are relatively higher, low and
middle  income  countries  have  even  lower  results,  such  as  Ethiopia
(24.6), Malaysia (24.2) and China (17.2).85,86,87 This disparity can be
explained by multiple factors, including limited access to digital devices
and  poor  internet  infrastructure,  which  make  it  difficult  to  actively
search for health information.

Our results show that the group with the lowest literacy is charac-
terized  predominantly  by  females.  These  results  align  with  previous
studies,  which  show  a  higher  level  for  men.34,88,89,52,33 Other  studies
from different countries show no significant differences in literacy levels
between  genders.51,90 Others  show  the  female  gender  with  the
highest.31,32,50

The fact that using YouTube to gather health information impacts the
eHEALS score negatively, although not always presenting as significant
in the regression, coupled with the amount of fake news and misinfor-
mation  on  the  platform91,92,93 shows  that  these  people  might  be

shared on social networks and online video platforms can compromise

Some  of  the  coefficients  in  our  models  have  presented  varying  p-

picked up by other variables, such as the use of private healthcare or
schooling levels, indicating a possible omitted-variable bias on the first
models, which was then corrected with the fixed effects.97,98 Another
aspect that plays a role here is that some variables have presented a low
number of respondents, such as those with primary schooling and those
who use Instagram as their main source of information online.

The  widespread  dissemination  of  misinformation  on  digital  plat-
forms  can have  significant  consequences  on  health  behaviors and  de-
cisions.  Studies  show  that  exposure  to  inaccurate  health  information
impairs  individuals’  ability  to  discern  reliable  content  and  promotes
behaviors that deviate from evidence-based practices.99,100,101,102 This
is particularly concerning during public health crises, as the spread of
false information can jeopardize the acceptance of vaccines and other
critical  preventive  measures  and  may  also  contribute  to  the  develop-
ment  of  psychological  disorders  such  as  panic,  fear,  and  depres-
sion.103,104 Considering  the  COVID-19  pandemic  and  the  information
crisis experienced, investigating and improving the population’s digital
health  literacy  levels  are  fundamental  factors  in  addressing  future
infodemics.105

Do Nascimento et al.106 show that the prevalence of health misin-
formation on social media varies considerably, with platforms such as
Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, and Instagram playing a significant role in
its rapid dissemination. According to the authors, the adverse effects of
misinformation  include  misinterpretation  of  evidence,  impacts  on
mental health, misallocation of resources, and increased vaccine hesi-
tancy. In addition, they emphasize the need for awareness campaigns,
improved  media  content,  and  strengthened  digital  health  literacy  to
mitigate the harmful effects of infodemics.

The  use  of  government  websites  to  access  health  information  is
positively correlated with digital health literacy, as shown in our model
with fixed effects and clustered errors, which corroborates the fact that
these are trustworthy sources of health information online. This result
highlights  the  importance  of  investing  in  government  websites,107
making  them  more  attractive and  user-friendly  for  the  population, to
mitigate  the  impact  of  misinformation  in  other  digital  sources.  These
official platforms have a fundamental role in correcting misinformation,
and it is essential to enforce the accessibility and quality of these web-
sites to ensure access to validated and scientifically supported content
online.108,109

The relationship between health literacy and digital health literacy is
close, and strategies to improve HL can also contribute significantly to
enhancing DHL. Formal education has already been discussed, including
health literacy in the school curriculum. It can provide individuals with
fundamental skills and knowledge to understand and use health infor-

informal learning is also essential, especially for the significant number

of adults with low levels of HL, a reality in many low-income countries.
Educational  programs  in  community  settings,  such  as  public  libraries
and local centers, or specific initiatives, such as training on digital health
technologies, can broaden access to knowledge and reduce barriers to
online services.115,116,117

The  literature  presents  various  interventions  to  improve  digital
health literacy, with special attention to the elderly population. Studies
such  as  those  by  Xie118–121 and  Nahm  et  al.122 highlight  approaches

navigation.  In  addition,  small  group  training  programs,  combining
classroom practice and educational support, showed a positive impact

Given these examples and the fact that a third of our sample has low
Digital  health  literacy,  government  interventions  should  prioritize
expanding internet access and improving technological infrastructure,
particularly in vulnerable areas, to ensure accessibility and inclusion, as
well as creating ways to provide trustworthy health information digi-
tally. Additionally, our findings indicate that digital health literacy is
positively associated with higher levels of education, higher income, and
the  use  of  the  private  health  system.  These  results  demonstrate  how
socioeconomic determinants influence the ability to access, understand,
and use digital health information, emphasizing the need for investment
in  fundamental areas such as improving the  quality of education and
creating  ways  to  reduce  the  impact  of  health  misinformation  on  the
internet.

Interventions to mitigate misinformation can have multiple forms,
but making them accessible to at-risk populations, such as seniors and
those with lower educational levels, is highly important. Studies have
shown  that  interactive  apps  focused  on  identifying  fake  news,  also
known  as  prebunking,  can
increase  resistance  to  misinforma-
tion.124,125,126 Community-engaged learning programs also had a posi-
tive effect on the digital health literacy of low-income older adults while
also  fostering  the  capacity  of  students  to  work  with  older  adults.127
Other  studies  have  shown  that  both  face-to-face  and  web-based  in-
terventions were successful in increasing digital health literacy of older
adults, with the former being more successful.128

It is important to recognize that while our analysis included a diverse
sample from various high-traffic urban locations in the Federal District,
our collection was limited to urban, high-traffic public areas within the
Federal District, and, as such, it did not include respondents living in
other regions of Brazil and in rural contexts. Therefore, it is important to
consider that regional particularities also impact digital health literacy
dynamics when extrapolating our results to other parts of the country.
Future studies could examine the role of digital device access to access
health  information  and  healthcare,  especially  in  rural  areas.  Further
research  on  digital  health  literacy  in  other  regions  of  Brazil  is  also
relevant to understanding the specific challenges of each area.

4.1. Conclusion

We found that one-third of the participants exhibit low digital health
literacy, with self-declared whites and males displaying higher levels.
This finding reflects the significant influence of sociodemographic fac-
tors, such as education and income, on digital literacy, highlighting a
universal  trend  of  inequality  in  access  to  online  health  information.
However, our study shows that these are not the only important factors.
The active search for online information also proved to be fundamental,
another aspect that impacts this issue is the quality of the online sources
being accessed. We identified that the use of less reliable platforms, such
as  YouTube,  is  negatively  correlated  with  digital  health  literacy,
demonstrating that, besides having access, it is essential that the infor-
mation  is  correct  and  well-curated.  Thus,  our  research  reinforces  the
need for public policies that, in addition to promoting digital inclusion,
ensure the dissemination of high-quality health information.

More  research  is  needed  to  explore  the  particularities  of  digital
health  literacy  in  other  Global  South  countries,  especially  in  Latin
America,  considering  the  scarcity  of  evidence  on  the  subject.  The

scenario  of  socioeconomic  inequalities,  limited  access  to  digital  tech-
nologies,  and  educational  disparities  in  many  of  these  countries  un-
derscores  the  importance  of  a  deeper  understanding  of  the  specific
challenges each country faces. Our study, by emphasizing the need for
governmental  interventions,  highlights  specific  subgroups  that  can
better  benefit  from  initiatives  such  as  interactive  prebunking  in-
terventions  as  well  as  web-based  and  community-based  activities
focused on increasing digital health literacy.
