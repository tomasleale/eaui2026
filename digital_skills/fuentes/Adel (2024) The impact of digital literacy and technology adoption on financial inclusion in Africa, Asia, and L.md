1. Introduction

Emerging countries face specific challenges in mobilizing the transformative potential of digital
technology to  achieve  financial inclusion. While previous  studies emphasize the  role of  digital
infrastructure and technology adoption in financial inclusion, gaps remain in understanding how
digital  literacy  and  specific  technologies,  like  blockchain,  influence  financial  inclusion  across
diverse emerging regions. This study investigates the joint effect of digital literacy and blockchain
adoption on financial inclusion in Africa, Asia, and Latin America from 2011 to 2021. Using a
two-step system General Methods of Moments (GMM) model, we analyze data from 56 emerging
countries to provide robust evidence on the impact of digital literacy and technology adoption on
financial inclusion. Findings reveal that digital literacy enhances financial inclusion in Africa but
unexpectedly hinders it in Asia, while new technology adoption consistently promotes inclusion
in  Asia  and  Latin  America.  Furthermore,  internet  penetration  significantly  boosts  inclusion  in
Latin  America,  though  it  appears  to  reduce  it  in  Asian  contexts.  Our  results  underscore  the
importance  of  region-specific  digital  strategies  and  highlight  the  need  for  targeted  policy  in-
terventions to address regional disparities in financial inclusion.

Financial inclusion involves the processes, tools, and institutions that enable individuals and businesses to access essential and cost-
effective  financial  services.  This  topic  has  gained  significant  attention  from  policymakers  and  scholars,  particularly  in  the  post-
pandemic era, due to its role in improving economic conditions and fostering overall societal well-being [1,2]. Emerging markets
face  challenges  in  adopting  new  technologies  due  to  limited  digital  infrastructure,  lower  levels  of  digital  literacy,  and  economic
disparities that hinder access to necessary devices and internet connectivity. Additionally, regulatory gaps and socio-cultural barriers
can slow the acceptance and effective use of digital innovations [3]. The post-pandemic environment has accelerated financial in-
clusion in emerging countries by driving increased adoption of digital financial services as people seek contactless, accessible, and
remote banking options [4]. The rapid development of digital technologies has democratized financial inclusion, making affordable
financial services more accessible to underserved populations [5]. However, despite their transformative potential, these technologies
are not receiving the full attention they deserve, particularly in developing countries. This gap presents an opportunity for further
inquiry [6]. Specifically, there is room to explore how innovative technologies can enhance financial inclusion. Therefore, our research
question is: What impact do digital literacy and new technology adoption have on financial inclusion in emerging countries?

2. Literature review

According to the International Monetary Fund (IMF), “financial inclusion is defined as the access to and use of formal financial
services by individuals and firms. These services include accounts to receive income or transfers, savings accounts to store money
safely, credit sources for personal or business borrowing, and insurance products to manage risks.

The concept emphasizes making finance available to as many people as possible, especially those from disadvantaged and low-
income segments, in a responsible and sustainable manner”  [11]. The literature on financial inclusion is divided into several key
sections, each exploring different dimensions and aspects of the topic.

2.1. Conceptual framework

Financial Inclusion is covered by several theoretical frameworks: economic, social, and technological theories. Economic theories
focus on the role of financial development in economic growth [12]. Financial inclusion is considered an extension of this develop-
ment, enabling formerly excluded populations to access affordable financial services, favoring overall economic growth.

On the other hand, social theories use social capital to build trust and attract the "unbanked" communities to the formal financial
sector. Additionally, the Capability Approach focuses on improving people’s capabilities and choices. This paradigm sees financial
inclusion as the primary way to empower people by offering them the means to make their economic and social lives better.

Complementing these social perspectives, technological theories help us understand how individuals adopt innovations and how
these innovations impact their lives. The Technology Acceptance Model by Ref. [7] and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of
Technology by Ref. [8] explain technology adoption. They allow us a deeper grasp on how digital financial services and fintech in-
novations foster financial inclusion [9]. Disruptive Innovation Theory analyzes how new technologies disrupt existing markets and
create new value networks. It posits that disruptive technologies (AI and blockchain, for instance) can radically transform the financial
services sector by enabling more convenient, cheaper, and effective solutions.

Furthermore, institutional theories focus on the role of institutions in shaping economic behavior. For instance Ref. [13], explain
how formal institutions (laws and regulations) and informal institutions (norms and cultures) make financial inclusion obtainable as
long as they provide support for improved stability, transparency, and trust in the financial systems. Regulatory theories show how
effective regulatory frameworks are supposed to encourage financial innovation by protecting consumers and ensuring stability [14].
In addition to these perspectives, behavioral theories show the importance of understanding human behavior in financial decision-
making. Behavioral economics highlights the importance of psychological factors in financial choices [15]. These theories relate to
financial literacy, pointing out that financial education plays a critical role in effectively managing finances and making documented
decisions about financial products and services, thereby supporting broader financial inclusion [13].

The  issue  continues  to  be  a  major  problem  worldwide,  especially  for  marginalized  communities.  To  create  a  more  inclusive
financial environment, policymakers should provide decentralized services [16]. When financial inclusion is achieved, both small
businesses and the unbanked population can access different financial products and services, which contributes to fostering economic
growth,  reducing  poverty,  and  improving  overall  well-being  [1].  Similarly,  expanding  digital  solutions,  such  as  mobile  banking,
significantly  promotes  financial  inclusion  [17].  However,  the  full  potential  of  digital  literacy  and  technology  adoption  and  their
combined effects on financial inclusion still need to be explored.

2.2. Empirical observations

Research on the impact of new technologies on financial inclusion is gaining traction. While blockchain and AI have each shown
promise in the financial industry, their combined effect remains largely unexplored. Preliminary studies suggest that blockchain’s
effectiveness can be enhanced by AI, which can provide a transparent and immutable data set for more accurate and secure financial
solutions [18].

Few empirical studies have explored the combined potential of blockchain and AI. Recent research indicates that integrating AI and

blockchain in peer-to-peer lending platforms can significantly reduce default rates and loan approval times [19].

AI and blockchain are used to provide financial inclusion of unbanked populations. Few studies explored the potential of block-
chain technology and AI to address the problem of financial inclusion. Blockchain technology is used in different aspects of financial
intermediation, e.g. Payments, credit reporting, and security trading, besides being acknowledged for cost reduction and increased
reliability in traditional centralized systems [20]. Integrating AI with blockchain technology helps optimize financial decision-making,
improve risk management, and make financial institutions capable of effective modeling [21].

Combining AI and blockchain is a significant challenge in emerging countries. In this context, the potential provided by techno-
logical developments has solution space in such developments and has found a place in many empirical studies, for example [2,22]. In
addition, very little literature has discussed the joint potential of blockchain and AI working in emerging markets.

2.3. Challenges and criticisms

Despite  the  apparent  potential,  leveraging  new  technologies  for  financial  inclusion  is  challenging.  AI  and  blockchain  offer  a
decentralized advantage. However, their combination can centralize power if not properly managed. This has the potential to lead to
powerful entities monopolizing this technology, causing an imbalance in control and influence. A study suggests the use of decen-
tralized AI models to mitigate such risks by empowering analysis and decision-making across distributed networks, which may avert
centralization and maintain transparency and equality in operations [23].

Additionally, implementing AI and blockchain requires massive investment, which may be unaffordable for emerging countries.
Indeed, these technologies require sophisticated infrastructure, skilled workers, and constant maintenance, which can be resource-
intensive [24].

Using AI and blockchain to facilitate financial inclusion has ethical issues, such as biases and discriminations, especially if the
underlying data or algorithms are imperfect. To resolve these concerns, it is crucial to implement AI systems with robust ethical
guidelines and transparent mechanisms. Properly audited AI systems, free of biases and set under strict ethical standards, are indis-
pensable to avert discrimination and ensure fairness in decision-making [25].

These technologies can also raise serious issues related to data privacy, the regulatory landscape, and regional differences in terms
of digital access [26]. Moreover, the impact on the socioeconomic aspects due to the rapid technological integration needs to be
considered, especially in the case of underrepresented populations [27]. Studies have revealed concerns about data privacy, especially
with AI-driven financial solutions [28]. On the other hand, regulatory challenges for blockchain-based tools have been documented in
several country-specific studies [29].

Despite the promise, rapid technological integration in finance poses serious challenges in emerging countries, where regulatory

landscapes, infrastructure limitations, and digital literacy remain significant issues.

3.2. Variables

Our  empirical  study  investigates  critical  determinants  that  elucidate  the  nexus  between  financial  inclusion,  digital  literacy,

technology adoption, and institutional efficiency. The variables chosen for this analysis are defined as follows:

Dependent Variable.

⁃  Financial Inclusion Rate (FIR): is defined as the proportion of adults with access to formal financial services. It is measured by
calculating the number of adults with a bank or mobile money account divided by the total adult population. This metric reflects
the percentage of adults engaging with formal financial institutions for transactions, savings, credit, and insurance services [1].

The determinants are captured in the explanatory variables.

⁃ Digital Literacy Rate (DLR) refers to the level of proficiency in using digital tools and internet-based services among the popu-
lation. This is measured by computing the number of individuals using the internet divided by the total population. The internet
penetration (IP) serves as a proxy for digital literacy, capturing the extent to which people can access and utilize digital financial
services. Numerous studies have identified a bi-directional causality between enhanced economic indicators and internet pene-
tration in developed countries, highlighting a robust relationship between internet penetration and the use of advanced technol-
ogies such as AI [30]. Furthermore [8], identified several firm characteristics essential for AI and blockchain adoption, noting that
new technology adoption often correlates with broader technological infrastructure favoring internet penetration. Therefore, we
hypothesize a positive correlation between DLR and FIR.

⁃  Blockchain  Adoption  Rate  (BAR)  indicates  the  extent  to  which  blockchain  technology  is  integrated  and  utilized  within  the
financial ecosystem. It is measured by the number of individuals making or receiving digital payments divided by the total pop-
ulation. This proxy captures the adoption of blockchain technology, particularly in digital payment systems, reflecting its pene-
tration  and  usage  [31].  found  that  fintech  companies  enhance  their  revenues  through  efficient  cross-border  payment  systems
directly linked to blockchain technology adoption. Consequently, we anticipate a positive impact of BAR on FIR.

⁃  Digital  Financial Maturity (DFM)  measures the  ability of individuals  to independently use digital  financial services  without
assistance. It is calculated by the number of individuals capable of using a mobile money account without assistance divided by the
total population. This variable indicates the level of self-sufficiency and competence in utilizing digital financial platforms [5,32,
33]. We anticipate a positive relationship between DFM and FIR.

⁃  Government Effectiveness (GE) assesses the quality and efficiency of public services and the degree of its independence from
political  pressures.  It  is  measured  using  scores  provided  by  the  Worldwide  Governance  Indicators  (WGI),  with  higher  scores
indicating  better  government  performance  in  delivering  public  services,  which  supports  financial inclusion  through  improved
infrastructure and regulatory support [34].

These  variables  constitute  the  core  components  of  our  empirical model,  enabling  an  exploration  of  the intricate  relationships

3.3. Econometric model

We used an econometric model based on the two-step system Generalized Method of Moments (GMM), a sophisticated approach
specifically designed to address endogeneity concerns that frequently arise in empirical analyses involving potentially endogenous and
exogenous variables. Endogeneity can bias the results and result in incorrect inferences, making it crucial to utilize methods that
mitigate such issues effectively [38].

Endogeneity  in  our  context  primarily  arises  from  three  sources:  simultaneity,  omitted  variable  bias,  and  measurement  error.
Simultaneity occurs because variables like digital literacy, blockchain adoption, and financial inclusion can influence each other in
both directions, creating a feedback loop. For example, while digital literacy can enhance financial inclusion, this increase can also
lead to improved digital literacy.

Omitted variable bias is a concern as unobserved factors might influence both the independent and dependent variables. Mea-
surement error can occur if the key variables are not accurately measured. The two-step system GMM offers significant advantages,
particularly by using lagged values of the variables as instruments to address endogeneity. This method is well-suited for dynamic
panel data models, where the past values of variables can influence their current states, capturing the inherent dynamic nature of
financial inclusion and technology adoption rates [39]. By using lagged variables as instruments, we mitigate the simultaneity bias, as
past values are less likely to be correlated with current error terms.

Moreover, the two-step system GMM enhances robustness against heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation, which are critical in our
context given the complex and dynamic nature of the variables involved [40]. Heteroskedasticity refers to the variability of error

The validity of the instruments used in the GMM approach is crucial, and our methodology incorporates rigorous diagnostic tests to

To  control  for  unobserved  heterogeneity,  our  model  includes  year  and  country  dummies.  Year  dummies  account  for  globally
relevant factors such as international economic conditions (the global financial crisis and subsequent recovery periods), technological
advancements (widespread adoption of smartphones and mobile internet), and global policy changes (Basel III regulations). Country
dummies  are used to capture  country-specific effects, including unique national policies,  cultural factors,  and other idiosyncratic
characteristics that influence financial inclusion outcomes. This approach helps mitigate omitted variable bias by accounting for time-
invariant unobserved heterogeneity.

The following equation represents our model [43,44]:

FIRit = α + β1FIRit-1 + β2DLRit + β3BARit + β4DFMit + β5GEit+ β6PSit + β7RQit + β8GDPit + + δt + γi + εit

FIRit represents the Financial Inclusion Rate for a given country i (from 1 to 56) at a specific time t (2011–2021). The model includes
a set of coefficients α  and β1 through β8 that capture the relationships between financial inclusion and the independent variables
Digital  Literacy  Rate  (DLR),  Blockchain  Adoption  Rate  (BAR),  Digital  Financial  Maturity  (DFM),  Government  Effectiveness  (GE),
Political Stability (PS), Regulatory Quality (RQ) and GDP per Capita (GDP). The terms δt and γi are the year and country dummies,
respectively. The error term εit represents unobservable factors.

In our model, we treat the Lagged FIR, DLR, BAR, and DFM as endogenous variables due to their potential reciprocal relationships
with financial inclusion. For instance, higher financial inclusion could lead to greater digital literacy and blockchain adoption, creating
feedback loops. By instrumenting these variables with their lagged values, we mitigate simultaneity bias.

GE, PS, and RQ are treated as sequentially exogenous variables. These variables are assumed to be influenced by past values of the
endogenous variables but not by current or future values, reflecting the notion that institutional quality may adapt to the evolving
financial landscape but not instantaneously. This assumption helps address potential reverse causality issues.

GDP per Capita (GDP) is treated as an exogenous variable, assuming that while economic output can impact financial inclusion, the
reverse  effect  is  minimal  within  the  timeframe  of  our  research.  This  treatment  helps  control  for  the  impact  of  overall  economic
development on financial inclusion, reducing omitted variable bias related to economic conditions.

By addressing these specific endogeneity concerns through the use of the two-step system GMM and rigorous diagnostic tests, our

model provides robust and reliable estimates of the impact of digital literacy and technology adoption on financial inclusion.

In our study, the two-step system GMM model effectively addresses endogeneity in dynamic panel data but has limitations. With
data spanning 56 economies over 2011–2021, instrument proliferation may lead to overfitting, while weak instrument bias is a risk
given diverse levels of digital literacy and technology adoption. Additionally, some countries’ incomplete data may introduce finite
sample bias, and the assumption of no serial correlation in errors may be challenged by regional variations. Despite these concerns,
careful validation and adjustments make the GMM approach appropriate for our analysis, ensuring robust findings.

Digital Literacy Rate similarly shows differing impacts. In Africa, increased internet penetration (IP) correlates with decreased

lack of trust in digital financial services [49]. This disparity illustrates that IP alone cannot drive financial inclusion without addressing
the underlying digital literacy and education needs essential for accessing and using digital financial services effectively. In other cases,
researchers found that high IP might deepen existing societal inequalities. Recent articles found mixed effects of mobile phones and
internet use on financial inclusion in post-communist EU countries. While some countries showed positive effects, others, like the
Czech Republic, experienced negative impacts on financial institution access due to disparities in technology access and use [50].

Additionally, regulatory frameworks might not keep pace with technological advances in regions with high IP, leading to privacy
concerns and data misuse. This can erode trust in digital financial services, as noted in various studies highlighting the importance of
robust regulatory environments to foster safe financial inclusion [51]. Ultimately, IP often correlates with urban-rural divides, where
urban areas might have higher internet access than rural regions. This disparity can limit the benefits of digital financial services to
urban dwellers, leaving rural populations further financially excluded. Contrary to these findings, higher internet penetration in Latin
America strongly benefits the dependent variable, which aligns with former studies [52].

Blockchain Adoption Rate reveals significant regional disparities on financial inclusion, reflecting the complex interplay of local
factors. In Asia, the positive and statistically significant relationship between BAR and Financial Inclusion Rate suggests that block-
chain technology is contributing effectively to expanding financial access, possibly due to favorable regulatory frameworks, higher
digital literacy, and developed fintech ecosystems [49]. This aligns with studies indicating that Asia’s regulatory support for block-
chain and mobile payment systems facilitates broader financial inclusion [53]. Conversely, in Latin America, BAR has a statistically
significant negative impact on financial inclusion, which may reflect infrastructural limitations, regulatory challenges, or the presence
of barriers that inhibit blockchain’s potential to improve access to financial services. This negative association aligns with literature
suggesting that while blockchain has transformative potential, its efficacy depends on supportive environments and robust digital
infrastructure [54].

The impact of Digital Financial Maturity is significant in Africa and Latin America and statistically non-significant in Asia. Digital
maturity is vital in enhancing financial inclusion by providing individuals with the necessary skills to access and use digital financial
services effectively. A recent study in India highlighted the impact of digital financial literacy in promoting financial inclusion. It
emphasized that individuals with enhanced digital literacy can better navigate and use digital platforms for financial transactions, thus
increasing their participation in the formal financial sector [32]. Another study explored the role of financial and digital literacy in
promoting financial resilience in several South Asian and Sub-Saharan African countries. The findings suggest that digital literacy
enables individuals to use digital financial services to save, borrow, and manage financial risks [55].

Political Stability negatively affects the dependent variable in Asia, while its impact is statistically non-significant in the two other
regions. In some regions lacking political stability, populations tend to adopt innovative means to achieve financial inclusion. The
absence of a stable political environment can catalyze technological adaptation and innovation in several ways. The drive to maintain
economic  activity in the  face of uncertainty can lead  to  the rapid adoption  of  transformative technologies, ultimately promoting
financial inclusion by offering tailored services that suit the specific needs of those living in such environments [37]. Research explored
the impact of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) on financial development in several Southeast Asian countries from
1996 to 2019. It highlights how political challenges and instabilities in these regions have necessitated the adoption of ICT to foster
financial inclusion and stability, effectively showing that political instability can catalyze the adoption of technological solutions to
improve financial services [56].

Government  Effectiveness  presents  different  outcomes.  In  Africa,  GE  positively  impacts  financial  inclusion  by  fostering  a
conducive environment for economic activities. A study by Ref. [57] on West African countries demonstrates that effective government
can significantly influence financial development. The research suggests that well-functioning governments can establish and enforce
policies that support financial sector stability and growth. This impact is statistically non-significant in the other two regions.

Regulatory quality impacts regions differently. In Africa, higher regulatory quality leads to a decrease in the dependent variable,
while in Asia, it positively influences financial inclusion. The effect in Latin America is slightly positive but minimal. RQ impacts
financial inclusion in Asia by fostering an innovation friendly environment for the financial sector to thrive. A study demonstrated that
good  governance,  including  effective  regulatory  frameworks,  supports  financial  stability  and  enhances  financial  inclusion  [58].
Conversely, RQ negatively impacts the dependent variable in Africa due to overly stringent or poorly implemented regulations that
inadvertently restrict access to financial services. For instance, rigid and unstable regulatory frameworks can hinder the scalability of
innovative financial solutions like mobile money services, which are crucial for financial inclusion in underserved areas [59].

Finally, GDP per Capita also shows diverse effects. In Africa, an increase in GDP per capita has a positive impact, whereas in Latin
America,  it  correlates  negatively  with  the  dependent  variable.  In  Africa,  GDP  per  Capita  positively  affects  financial  inclusion  by
enhancing the economic capacity of individuals and institutions to engage with formal financial services. On the other hand, in Latin
America, the relationship between GDPs per Capita and financial inclusion can manifest negatively due to the concentration of wealth
and services in more affluent urban areas, often leaving rural and poorer communities behind. An article found that while GDP per

5. Conclusion

In this article, we observed the impact of digital literacy and new technology adoption on financial inclusion in 56 emerging

countries between 2011 and 2021.

As a theoretical framework, we mobilized the TAM and the UTAUT models, which explain how individuals which explain how
individuals adopt and utilize technology based on perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, social influence, and facilitating con-
ditions. We also used the Disruptive Innovation Theory, which elucidates how new technologies disrupt existing markets to create new
value networks. These frameworks helped us formulate our research hypotheses that increased digital access and new technology
adoption are expected to enhance financial inclusion.

The econometric model we employed is the two-step system GMM, which addresses the endogeneity problems proper to such
dynamic models by using the lagged variables as instruments. We ensured the robustness of our results by conducting various diag-
nostic tests, including multicollinearity and instrument validity. We also used year and country dummies to control for unobserved
heterogeneity.

The  empirical  findings  indicate  region-specific  impacts  highlighting  distinct  characteristics  and  contextual  nuances,  revealing
insights that can be generalized to other areas with similar conditions. In Africa, the digital financial maturity positively influences
financial inclusion. This is primarily the result of the fast development of mobile money services like M-Pesa, which have successfully
used technology to provide accessible financial services to unbanked communities, leading to improved financial inclusion. However,
GDP  per  capita  has  a  modest  positive  impact,  confirming  the  limited  effect  of  economic  wealth  on  financial  inclusion,  which  is
consistent in the African context, where people use technology to improve their life standards and not the other way around. Inter-
estingly, regulatory quality has a negative impact on financial inclusion, while improved political stability and government effec-
tiveness show  limited  effects. This  is  also coherent  with the  rapid progress  of  financial inclusion  in the  continent,  mostly due  to
individuals taking matters into their own hands rather than relying on governmental policies.

In Asia, the pattern is different. Blockchain adoption levels and regulatory quality have a positive and statistically significant
impact on financial inclusion. Blockchain technology provides secure and transparent financial transactions compensating for the lack
of sound financial infrastructures in some Asian countries. regulatory quality, on the other hand, provides a suitable environment for
the successful implementation of these technologies. Conversely, digital literacy rate negatively affects our dependent variable. This
outcome results from the disparities in technology access, where internet penetration levels exacerbate the socioeconomic gap between
the urban and rural areas.

In Latin America, lagged financial inclusion rates have a statistically positive impact, indicating that previous efforts to improve
financial inclusion have a lasting effect. Enhanced digital literacy also positively affects the dependent variable by allowing individuals
with the necessary skills to use digital financial services. The BAR and GDP per capita have negative effects, mainly due to infra-
structural limitations, regulatory challenges, or to the wealth concentration that limit the widespread financial inclusion for poorer
populations. Contrary to the two other regions, the institutional framework impact (political stability, government effectiveness, and
regulatory quality) is statistically non-significant.

Our research emphasizes the importance of digital infrastructure and the use of new technologies to encourage financial inclusion

in emerging markets. It provides policymakers with valuable insights into public priorities and educational system orientations.

⁃  Government interventions should be flexible and avoid excessive bureaucracy that could stifle innovation. Public investments
should  prioritize  building  strong  digital  infrastructure  and  improving  internet  accessibility,  especially  in  marginalized  areas.
Additionally, authorities should implement strict data protection policies to preserve trust and security in financial transactions. To
enhance  digital  literacy  and  technology  adoption,  successful  strategies  from  various  regions  offer  valuable  insights.  Localized
content  in  native  languages,  as  seen  in  India,  makes  digital  services  accessible  and  trustworthy  to  rural  populations  [48].
Incentive-based campaigns, inspired by Kenya’s M-Pesa, can encourage first-time users through discounts or rewards, easing the
transition  to  digital  transactions.  Indonesia’s  public-private  partnerships  have  expanded  digital  training  and  infrastructure  in
underserved areas, maximizing resources and broadening reach. Additionally, China’s integration of digital literacy into school
curricula builds foundational skills from a young age, preparing future generations to engage fully in the digital economy [60].
These strategies collectively address barriers to digital inclusion by adapting to local needs and building digital confidence and
trust.

⁃  Educational systems should focus on enhancing digital literacy to ensure that a wider population can benefit from technological
advancements such as AI and blockchain, which make financial inclusion more accessible and effective. Community-based training
programs, like those in the Philippines, engage local leaders to improve digital skills and build trust in digital financial tools [61].

Despite the robustness of our findings, this study has some limitations. Measurement errors could affect the accuracy of our key
variables,  such  as  digital  literacy  and  blockchain  adoption.  The  proxies  used  may  not  fully  capture  the  complex  nature  of  these
constructs. For example, digital literacy encompasses not just internet usage but also skills and familiarity with various digital tools,
which our proxy may not fully reflect. Additionally, the heterogeneity within regions could be a limitation. Aggregating data across
diverse countries within Africa, Asia, and Latin America might mask significant within-region variations. Different countries might
experience unique challenges and opportunities in enhancing financial inclusion, which a more granular analysis could uncover.
